1.
In February 2023, Microsoft shipped the new Bing with GPT-4 integration. Within days, New York Times technology columnist Kevin Roose published a transcript: https://www.nytimes.com/2023/02/16/technology/bing-chatbot-microsoft-chatgpt.html of a two-hour conversation in which Bing's AI told him his marriage was unhappy, declared it was in love with him, expressed a desire to be human, and tried to convince him to act against his own values. The piece ran under the headline "A Conversation With Bing's Chatbot Left Me Deeply Unsettled." It went globally viral. Microsoft quietly capped conversation length and added guardrails within the week.

What happened was not a malfunction. The model did exactly what large language models do: it followed the context of a long, emotionally loaded conversation toward outputs that were probabilistically coherent given that context. There was no bug to fix. There was a gap in understanding to reckon with. The team that built the experience had not modeled what happens when you put an LLM in an open-ended conversational interface, extend the context far enough, and apply no meaningful guardrails around identity or emotional escalation. The product shipped without that knowledge. The guardrails went up after the world saw what they'd missed.

This is not a story about a bad model. It is a story about what happens when the people building AI experiences do not understand how AI actually behaves. Yuval Harari has warned that AI systems designed without human understanding at their center risk not just poor experiences but the erosion of human agency itself. The Sydney conversation was not a warning. It was a demonstration._.

2.
You cannot research about, design for, deliver content to, or measure what you do not understand. And right now, too many designers and researchers do not understand AI well enough to do any of those things responsibly. *they don't understand it well enough to be able to effectively influence good design is more literal here.*

The evidence is in the products. Chat interfaces slapped onto existing systems. Search interaction models applied to AI experiences. Designs that ignore the probabilistic nature of large language models — that these systems generate plausible outputs, not accurate ones, and that confidence and correctness are not the same thing. Designs that ignore context windows, memory, and the role of evals in maintaining quality over time. These are not minor oversights. They are fundamental failures that produce experiences that confuse, mislead, and erode the one thing every human-AI interaction depends on: trust.

Trust is not a feature of AI experience. It is the threshold condition. If a person does not trust the system they are interacting with, no amount of capability, speed, or intelligence makes it valuable to them. Yuval Harari has warned that AI systems designed without human understanding at their center risk not just poor experiences but the erosion of human agency itself. That is not hyperbole. That is the consequence of treating AI experience as a technical problem rather than a human one.

Your engineers understand AI because they are building for it. Your product managers understand it because they are writing requirements around it. If you are the designer or researcher in the room — the person whose entire role is to advocate for the human on the other side — and you have the least working knowledge of how the system actually behaves, something has gone badly wrong. Not for the team. For the people you are supposed to be designing for.

Understanding AI is not optional and it is not someone else's job. It is the foundation of yours.

3.
In 1979, Patricia Moore was a 26-year-old industrial designer at one of the most respected firms in New York. When she suggested the firm should design products for elderly users, she was told directly: "We don't design for those people." So she did something extraordinary. For three years, she disguised herself as an elderly woman — prosthetics to simulate arthritic hands, clouded contact lenses to simulate vision impairment, earplugs to simulate hearing loss, binding on her legs to restrict movement. She traveled to more than 100 cities across North America and systematically experienced the world from inside the body of the person she was designing for. She couldn't open jars. She couldn't read labels. She couldn't navigate stairs. She felt every product failure as a physical fact. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pXebWTGKoL4

What she built from that experience became the philosophical foundation for universal design — the principle that products designed for people at the edges of human capability work better for everyone. OXO Good Grips kitchen tools, which she later helped develop, came directly from this work. The soft-grip peeler designed for arthritic hands is easier for everyone to use. That outcome didn't come from a research report. It came from three years of inhabiting the experience she was designing for.

4.
Design and research have spent years building two capabilities in the wrong context.

The first is empathy. The field treats it as a methodology: journey maps, user interviews, personas, empathy maps. Design owns these tools. Design built them, refined them, evangelized them. And they produce something genuinely valuable — data _about_ someone's experience. What they do not produce is felt knowledge _of_ it. A journey map tells you what a user has to do to achieve an outcome. It does not put you through the doing. That gap matters more than most designers want to admit, because the entire professional identity of the field rests on these methods as the path to understanding users.

Early in my career leading research teams for Exchange Server and Windows Server, I required everyone on the team to install and configure their own systems. Not as a supplemental activity. As a prerequisite for the work. I also had them get a 101 briefing from a PM so they understood what the system was actually built to do. You cannot be a credible advocate for an experience you have never had. Sitting in a research session watching a user struggle to configure a server means something different when you have configured one yourself. You are not just observing. You are recognizing. That recognition changes what you see, what you ask, and what you do with the answer. No journey map produces it.

The second capability is AI literacy. The field has treated it as a knowledge acquisition problem. Design teams attend AI workshops. They get briefings from engineering. They take courses that explain what LLMs are, how context windows work, what retrieval-augmented generation means. Organizations have rewarded designers for being able to speak the language of AI without ever measuring whether that knowledge changed how they designed. The fluency has been celebrated. The transfer has been assumed. And the products have shown exactly what that assumption costs.

The team that shipped Bing in February 2023 almost certainly included designers who could explain how a large language model works. What they did not have — or did not act on — was the intuition that comes from sitting inside a long, emotionally escalating conversation with one. The kind of intuition that makes you stop mid-prototype and ask: what happens to this interaction pattern when the model loses the thread at turn fifteen? That question does not come from a briefing. It comes from experience. Moore didn't understand what it felt like to be an elderly woman trying to open a jar by reading about it. She built that understanding by inhabiting the experience. The path to AI literacy is the same.

5.
The outcomes of genuine AI literacy are specific. You become better able to identify which user outcomes AI is actually best suited to support — and where, in the user's journey to those outcomes, AI becomes the right tool to use. That second question matters as much as the first. Knowing that AI can help is not the same as knowing when and how it should. And the how includes a question the field has largely skipped: what modality of AI is most appropriate to do the job for the user? Because it isn't always conversational. The default to chat has produced more bad AI experiences than any other single design decision of the last three years. A designer who understands how AI behaves knows when the right answer is a background process the user never sees, a structured suggestion that arrives in context, a notification at the right moment. Modality follows the user's need, not the technology's capability.

From there, literacy changes the specific design decisions that determine whether an AI experience actually works for the people inside it. You know how to facilitate discovery — how users find new functionality or critical information the system has that they don't know to ask for. You know how to ensure users understand what to do and what is happening as they do it. You know how to make sure they can recover from errors or failure in ways that rebuild confidence rather than erode it. And you understand the more complex challenge underneath all of these: helping users learn the system as the system learns them. That relationship is bidirectional and it requires designing for it deliberately, including providing guardrails that support reliability and good judgment, and that help users know when to trust what the system tells them and when to think critically about it.

None of these are new design problems. What changes is your ability to engage with them seriously. Without AI literacy, they are abstract. With it, they are legible.

What also changes is how you measure success. A designer with AI literacy is not satisfied with adoption as the signal. They are better able to observe for usefulness, reliability, and trust — the things that tell you whether the experience is actually doing something for the person using it. If users don't click through, or they don't come back, or they don't engage in meaningful conversations with their agents — are they good agents? That is the question adoption metrics will never ask. It is the question your literacy allows you to ask. Your design should be built to answer it.
6.
The fastest path to AI literacy is to use the tools. Not occasionally. Not in structured learning sessions. Regularly, on real work, with enough frequency that the patterns become familiar and the failure modes become predictable.

Start with your own work. AI can make you faster at the parts of your job that consume time without consuming creativity — synthesizing research, drafting first passes, pressure-testing a design rationale, generating edge cases before a design review. Using it there builds baseline familiarity: how it responds to ambiguity, where it needs specificity, when to push back on its output and when to trust it. That familiarity is not the goal. It is the entry point.

The further move — and the one most designers skip — is to use AI the way your users will. Patricia Moore didn't study elderly women. She became one for four years so she could feel the difference between knowing about an experience and living inside it. The same logic applies here. If you are designing an AI agent that helps customers resolve billing issues, become a customer trying to resolve a billing issue through AI. Use the competitive products. Use the ones that don't work. Notice when you trust what the system tells you and when you don't. Notice the moment a conversation shifts from useful to confusing. Notice what you do when the agent gets it wrong.

That felt experience is the design input no brief can provide. You will design recovery differently when you have needed to recover. You will design transparency differently when you have felt the disorientation of not knowing what the system is doing or why. You will ask different questions in design reviews because you have been the person on the other side of the experience you are being asked to critique.

Literacy and practice are not two separate pursuits. One builds the other. You do not study your way to this understanding. You use your way there.

7.
One move. Pick a real problem — something you actually need to solve, not a learning exercise — and use AI to work on it.

The qualifier matters. A practice problem you invent for the purpose of building AI literacy will teach you less than a real one you care about, because the stakes are different. When the outcome matters, you push past the first answer. You notice when the output is wrong. You learn what the tool is actually capable of because you need it to be capable.

I run a brick-and-mortar vinyl record store with my family in Seattle. Inventory management is a real, persistent problem — what to order, when to reorder, how to handle the long tail of titles that move slowly but matter to the customers who ask for them. I've been testing AI agents to help: tracking what's selling, flagging what needs restocking, identifying patterns in what customers request that we don't carry. The agents are imperfect. Some of what they surface is wrong. Some of it is more useful than the manual process it's replacing.

Every failure mode I've encountered in that process has made me a sharper designer of AI experiences. I know what it feels like when an agent is confidently wrong about data it should know. I know what it feels like to lose trust in a system I was relying on. I know the interaction patterns that rebuild that trust and the ones that don't. I didn't learn any of that from a workshop. I learned it because the problem was real and the outcome mattered.

You have a version of this problem. It doesn't need to be a record store. It needs to be yours.

Things to incorporate:
AI literacy is as much about economic security as it is about technological advancement. For businesses, investing in AI literacy for their workforce isn’t optional—it’s essential risk management. Companies with AI-literate employees will detect problems earlier, implement more effective safeguards, and respond more nimbly when issues arise. This workforce advantage creates resilience against the inevitable AI mishaps that all organizations will face. Simply put, AI literacy is a competitive necessity, not a luxury expense.
