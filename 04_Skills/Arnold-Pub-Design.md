# Arnold Publication Design Principles

## Applied to Tammy Snow's Written Work

  

This file translates Edmund Arnold's empirically grounded publication design framework into concrete instructions for structuring Tammy's written artifacts. Arnold — considered the father of modern newspaper design — based every principle on readability research, not aesthetic preference. That alignment with evidence-first thinking makes his framework a natural complement to TAM.

  

These are not style preferences. They are the structural conditions that determine whether a reader stays or leaves before the argument lands.

  

---

  

## The Five Principles — Applied

  

### 1 · Readability over aesthetics

  

Every structural and formatting decision is tested against one question: does this make it easier or harder for the reader to absorb the content?

  

**In practice:**

- Body text in documents: 11–12pt, 1.5× line height, high contrast (Charcoal `#2D2D2D` on Stone `#F5F0EB` or white)

- Column width controlled to 50–75 characters per line — this is the physiological limit of comfortable lateral eye movement. Wider than 75 characters and the reader loses the return path. Narrower than 45 and rhythm breaks with hyphens.

- One typeface family throughout. Hierarchy through weight and size, not face variety.

- Decoration earns its place by serving comprehension or navigation. If it does neither, it goes.

  

**Flag and fix:**

- Any reversed type (light text on dark background) in body copy — acceptable for display moments only, never sustained reading

- Font sizes below 11pt in body contexts

- Line height below 1.4 — lines blur into each other

- More than two typeface families in a single document

  

**The test:** Cover the visual styling. Does the structure still work? If not, the design is doing work the content should be doing.

  

---

  

### 2 · The Gutenberg Diagram — place content where the eye goes

  

Arnold's Gutenberg Diagram maps how the eye moves across a page or screen in left-to-right reading cultures:

  

```

┌─────────────────────────────────────┐

│ PRIMARY OPTICAL AREA │ Strong │

│ (top-left) — START │ Fallow │

│ Eye enters here │ │

├──────────────────────────┼──────────│

│ Weak Fallow │ TERMINAL │

│ (bottom-left) │ AREA │

│ Almost no attention │ END/CTA │

└──────────────────────────┴──────────┘

```

  

The eye enters top-left, sweeps left-to-right across the page, and terminates bottom-right. The two fallow areas — top-right and bottom-left — receive significantly less natural attention.

  

**In practice for Tammy's work:**

  

| Content type | Placement |

|---|---|

| The lead argument, most critical claim | Top-left — the Primary Optical Area |

| Primary call to action, key link, next step | Bottom-right — the Terminal Area |

| Secondary navigation, dates, bylines | Top-right — Strong Fallow (gets some attention) |

| Methods, footnotes, supplementary context | Bottom-left — Weak Fallow (read by the committed) |

| Hero image or anchor visual | Top-left quadrant — earns its POA position |

  

**Flag and fix:**

- Newsletters with the primary offer or CTA below the fold in the bottom-left

- Executive communications where the ask is buried in the final paragraph (bottom-left of a text document)

- Thought leadership pieces that bury the thesis in the third paragraph — the POA is the opening sentence

- Slide decks where the most important data point appears in the bottom-left corner of a chart

  

**The rule:** If someone reads only the top-left corner of your page — your first sentence, your headline, your opening visual — do they know what you're arguing and why it matters? If not, the most important content is misplaced.

### 3 · The Axis of Orientation — give the eye a reliable return point

  

After every left-to-right reading sweep, the eye needs to know exactly where to re-enter. That return point is the Axis of Orientation — almost always the left margin. Any layout that shifts, disrupts, or randomizes that axis forces the reader to hunt for re-entry on every line. The friction is invisible to the writer and cumulative for the reader.

  

**In practice:**

- Left-align all body text. Always. Center alignment is for display headlines and short labels only — never for sustained reading.

- Maintain consistent left margins across all columns in multi-column layouts.

- When indenting pull quotes or callout boxes, keep them anchored to the same column grid — offset within the grid, not outside it.

- In email newsletters: a single-column layout almost always outperforms multi-column for readability. Multi-column feels designed; single-column gets read.

- In slide decks: text blocks that begin at different x-positions on consecutive slides destroy the axis. Set a text origin and hold it.

  

**Flag and fix:**

- Center-aligned body paragraphs anywhere in a document (common in annual reports and executive presentations)

- Text wrapping around irregular image shapes — the axis becomes a moving target

- Multi-column layouts that switch column count mid-document

- Justified text in narrow columns — the word spacing variation destroys the return path more than it aids the axis

  

**The test:** Run your finger down the left edge of any text block. Is there a consistent vertical line? If the finger has to zigzag, the axis is broken.

### 4 · Hierarchy as navigation — signal before the reader commits

  

Readers skim before they read. Hierarchy is the system that tells a skimming reader what to commit to. It is a service function, not a decorative one. A document with no hierarchy asks the reader to hold everything at equal attention — which means they hold nothing.

  

**In practice — minimum viable hierarchy for Tammy's documents:**

  

| Level | Tammy's system | Function |

|---|---|---|

| Display | 40–52pt · Bold · Navy | Section identity — used sparingly |

| H1 | 28–32pt · Bold · Navy | Document or piece title — one per artifact |

| H2 | 22–24pt · Bold · Navy or Charcoal | Major section breaks |

| H3 | 18–20pt · SemiBold · Charcoal | Subsections — used when sections run >300 words |

| Body | 11–12pt · Regular · Charcoal · 1.5 line-height | Sustained reading |

| Labels | 8–9pt · Bold · Amber · Letter-spaced | Category tags, navigation elements |

| Caption | 9–10pt · Regular · Mid Gray | Supporting annotation only |

  

**Rules:**

- Minimum three visible size steps between headline and body. Two steps is not enough differentiation to function as navigation.

- One dominant visual entry point per page. If everything is bold, nothing is.

- Subheads every 200–300 words in documents over 500 words — this is the skim anchor that keeps a reader oriented even if they're moving at speed.

- Never use bold mid-sentence for decoration. Bold means "this is a navigational landmark or a critical term." Use it that way consistently.

  

**Flag and fix:**

- The wall-of-text executive memo — identical weight and size throughout, no visual entry points, no skim path

- Headers that are only marginally larger than body text (16pt headline, 14pt body — not enough)

- Bold used promiscuously throughout body text — when everything is emphasized, nothing is

- Thought leadership pieces with no subheads — even a 600-word LinkedIn essay benefits from a single mid-piece structural break

  

**The test:** Give the document to someone with 10 seconds. Ask them what the piece is about and what they should do with it. If they can't answer, the hierarchy failed.

### 5 · Typography as function — signals must be consistent and meaningful

  

Every typographic choice is a signal. Weight says "this matters." Size says "this is a different tier." Spacing says "breathe here." Color says "this is a different category." For those signals to work, they must mean the same thing every time they appear. Typography that decorates — using styles for variety or visual interest rather than consistent signal — produces a publication that feels rich but navigates poorly.

  

**In practice:**

- Bold means one thing: a navigational landmark or a critical term. Not decoration, not enthusiasm.

- Italic means one thing: a title, a foreign term, or a deliberate voice shift. Not general emphasis.

- Amber (`#D4870E`) means one thing: a call to action, a key data point, or maximum emphasis. One instance per section.

- Sienna (`#B85C38`) means one thing: the single most important moment in the document. One instance per composition.

- All-caps means one thing: a label. Never used in body copy.

  

**Rules:**

- No script fonts. Ever. In any context.

- No decorative typefaces in body or heading applications.

- No mixed serif/sans combinations — Inter or Aktiv Grotesk (Arial in Office environments) throughout.

- Weight range: Regular (400) to Bold (700) for most applications. Black (900) for display moments only.

- Underline is reserved exclusively for hyperlinks. Bold + color for all other emphasis.

  

**Flag and fix:**

- The "personality deck" — different headline font per slide to signal that each section is special. The effect is that the reader can't build a reading pattern.

- Italics used as general emphasis throughout body copy — at document scale, italic loses its signal value entirely

- Multiple accent colors used inconsistently — Amber on one section, Sienna on another, blue somewhere else. The reader can't decode the system.

- Decorative pull-quote styling that uses a completely different typeface from the rest of the document — the visual break signals a category shift that doesn't exist

  

**The test:** Without reading the content, can someone reverse-engineer the typographic system? "Bold = key term, Amber = CTA, H2 = new section." If they can, the typography is functional. If it just feels varied and interesting, it's decorative.

## Applied to Tammy's Specific Formats

  

### Newsletters (internal team or external thought leadership)

  

- Single-column layout. Always. Multi-column newsletters get scanned, not read.

- Lead with the thesis in the first sentence — the POA for a text document is the opening line.

- One primary CTA, placed at the end (Terminal Area). Not mid-newsletter, not twice.

- Subheads for any newsletter over 400 words.

- Subject line = the display headline. It should carry the argument, not describe the topic. "Design's role in AI ROI" is a topic. "Most AI implementations fail because design is an afterthought" is an argument.

  

### Executive communications and strategy documents

  

- Bottom line up front — the recommendation before the rationale. Always.

- Structure follows argument, not chronology. The reader needs to know the conclusion before they'll invest in the evidence.

- Three-tier hierarchy minimum: document title, section heads, body. No more complexity than the content requires.

- POA carries the ask or the headline finding. If someone reads only the first paragraph, they should know what you're proposing.

- The weak fallow (bottom-left, appendix, final section) is where methodology and supporting data live — not the recommendation.

  

### Thought leadership (LinkedIn, external publications)

  

- The first sentence is the entire argument in compressed form. It lives in the POA. It must work as a standalone claim.

- No warming up. No "I've been thinking about..." — that's burying the lead in the fallow area.

- Subhead if the piece exceeds 500 words. One subhead that carries the pivot or the counter-argument.

- The closing sentence is the Terminal Area. It should land the argument or open the reader into a question. Not summarize what was just said.

- Bold used once — on the most important sentence in the piece, only if the piece runs long enough to need a skim anchor.

  

### Slide decks (analyst presentations, executive briefings, customer events)

  

- One argument per slide — the slide title carries the claim, not the topic. "AI adoption" is a topic. "Adoption fails at the seam between AI and the human workflow" is an argument.

- The primary data point or visual lives top-left on the slide.

- The conclusion or CTA lives bottom-right.

- Consistent text origin across all slides — the axis holds across the deck.

- No more than three type sizes per slide. No script. No decorative faces.

## The Editorial Check — Before Any Document Leaves

  

Run these against Arnold's principles before publishing or sending:

  

1. **POA test** — Does the most important content sit in the top-left entry point? Is the first sentence, headline, or visual the strongest thing on the page?

2. **Axis test** — Is all body text left-aligned? Does the eye have a consistent return point on every line?

3. **Hierarchy test** — Can someone skim this in 10 seconds and know what it's about and what to do?

4. **Signal test** — Does every typographic choice mean the same thing every time it appears?

5. **CTA test** — Is the primary call to action or next step in the Terminal Area (bottom-right, final sentence), not buried in the weak fallow?

6. **Line length test** — Is body text running 50–75 characters per line? If it's spanning the full width of an A4 page or a wide screen without column control, it's too wide.
