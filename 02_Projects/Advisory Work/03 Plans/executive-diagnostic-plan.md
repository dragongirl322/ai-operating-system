## Executive Diagnostic & Advisory Practice

### Business Plan and 12-Month Roadmap

#### October 2026 – September 2027

### 1. The Business in One Sentence

I help executive teams determine what is actually preventing strategy from becoming results, identify the few decisions that matter most, and help leaders reshape the organization, operating model, or leadership system accordingly.

The business is built around judgment and diagnosis rather than implementation capacity.

The progression is:

Diagnose → Clarify → Decide → Advise → Learn → Codify

Over time, repeated engagements create a body of intellectual property around leadership, organizational effectiveness, AI-era operating models, customer-centered strategy, and transformation.

### 2. Why This Business Exists

Organizations frequently misdiagnose their own problems.

They believe they have:

an execution problem;

a product problem;

an AI problem;

a design problem;

a leadership problem;

an organizational structure problem;

a speed problem;

or a talent problem.

Often those are symptoms rather than causes.

The cost of acting on the wrong diagnosis is enormous. Companies reorganize, hire executives, install processes, build products, launch AI initiatives, or replace leaders without correcting the system that produced the problem in the first place.

My role is to enter complicated situations without an agenda about what the answer should be and determine:

What is really happening?

Why is it happening?

What matters versus what is noise?

Which few decisions will materially change the system?

What should leadership do next?

This is not conventional management consulting. It is not fractional executive work. It is not design consulting. And it is not traditional executive coaching.

It sits at the intersection of executive diagnosis, organizational strategy, operating-model design, product/customer thinking, and leadership advisory.

### 3. Why Me

I have spent nearly four decades in technology, more than 25 years leading people, and the last decade operating at executive scale.

I have built and led large multidisciplinary organizations across design, research, analytics, technology, product experience, and AI. I have worked through major organizational transformations, product shifts, leadership changes, and emerging technology transitions.

That experience matters because I have occupied the operating seat. I have lived with the consequences of executive decisions rather than advising from outside the system.

But operating experience alone is not the differentiator. My ability to diagnose complex organizations comes from four reinforcing sources.

#### 3.1 Operator credibility

I have led large organizations, transformations, teams, and enterprise initiatives. I understand the constraints leaders operate within: competing priorities, politics, incomplete information, talent realities, customer expectations, technical dependencies, financial pressure, and the difficulty of turning strategy into coordinated action.

I know the difference between an idea that is intellectually appealing and one that can actually survive contact with an organization.

#### 3.2 A system-level vantage point

My leadership roles at Expedia and Workday were intentionally horizontal. The teams I led worked across functions, products, business units, and organizational boundaries rather than inside a single vertical domain.

That gave me an unusually broad view of how the enterprise actually behaved as a system.

I repeatedly worked across product, engineering, design, research, data, business functions, executive leadership, and customer journeys. I could see how decisions in one part of the organization created consequences somewhere else, where handoffs broke down, where incentives competed, and where individually rational decisions produced collectively poor outcomes.

Those horizontal roles often required my organizations to provide the steel-thread connection across the enterprise: maintaining continuity between customer understanding, strategy, product decisions, organizational behavior, and the resulting experience.

That vantage point trained me to see problems as systems rather than isolated functional failures.

#### 3.3 A researcher's way of seeing

Although I am an executive and experience professional, I am a researcher at my core.

My natural mode is to observe before prescribing. I listen for what people say, notice what they do, identify recurring patterns, surface contradictions, construct hypotheses, infer underlying meaning, and test those interpretations against additional evidence.

That research orientation is especially useful in ambiguous organizational situations because the stated problem is often not the real problem. Leaders frequently have fragments of the truth distributed across functions, levels, data, customer signals, and lived experience. My work is to connect those fragments into a coherent explanation of what the system is producing and why.

#### 3.4 Executive judgment

Decades of operating, horizontal exposure, and research practice have created a large internal pattern library.

I can often recognize that something does not add up before the full explanation is obvious. The value is not intuition alone; it is the ability to turn that signal into a hypothesis, investigate it, distinguish evidence from assumption, and determine whether the pattern is real.

I am particularly effective at:

finding the problem underneath the stated problem;

synthesizing large amounts of ambiguous information;

identifying contradictions others have normalized;

connecting customer, business, organizational, leadership, and technical realities;

seeing the seams between functions and understanding their system effects;

distinguishing root causes from symptoms;

challenging assumptions without creating unnecessary theater;

turning complexity into a small number of consequential decisions;

and helping leaders act without becoming dependent on me to operate the business.

My particular strength is seeing relationships across systems that are often treated separately:

strategy ↔ customer ↔ product ↔ organization ↔ incentives ↔ leadership ↔ technology ↔ behavior

My value is therefore not simply what I know.

It is how I see.

### 5. Ideal Client

The ideal client is a **small to lower mid-market company at a consequential growth or transformation inflection point**, particularly one that is:

- private-equity owned;
- venture-backed and preparing for another stage of growth;
- seeking PE or VC investment;
- recently acquired;
- preparing for a recapitalization, exit, or significant growth phase;
- or under pressure to materially improve revenue, margin, customer value, or operating performance.
- 
These organizations are especially well suited to the practice because they frequently face a difficult transition:

**the operating model, leadership behaviors, product strategy, customer understanding, or organizational structure that got the company to its current stage will not necessarily get it to the next one.**

They may know they need to transform, but they often do not yet know precisely **what must change, why, or in what sequence**.

**Typical Client Profile**

The strongest-fit organization will generally have:

- enough scale and complexity that problems cross functional boundaries;
- a leadership team capable of acting once the real problem is understood;
- meaningful customer and revenue pressure;
- an explicit growth or value-creation thesis;
- limited tolerance for long consulting engagements;
- and a near-term reason to improve performance.

The organization may describe its challenge as:

- "We need to accelerate growth."
- "The company needs to become more scalable."
- "We need an AI strategy."
- "Our product organization isn't delivering."
- "We need to professionalize the company."
- "We need to become more customer-centric."
- "Execution is too slow."
- "The leadership team isn't aligned."
- "We need to transform before the next investment round."
- "The investment thesis isn't translating into operating results."

The diagnostic engagement determines whether those are the actual problems or symptoms of a deeper system-level issue.

**Why This Segment**

Small and lower mid-market organizations have an important combination of characteristics:

**They are complex enough to have systemic problems, but small enough that executive decisions can still materially change the company.**

In a Fortune 100 enterprise, even an excellent diagnosis may require years of organizational machinery to produce change.

In a $50M–$500M organization, the CEO and leadership team can often act directly on a diagnosis and produce observable results much faster.

That makes the segment particularly attractive for building both client value and evidence for the advisory methodology.

**Investor Context**

PE and VC ownership adds another important dimension.

The organization is not transforming for transformation's sake. There is usually an explicit economic objective:

**grow revenue, improve margin, increase enterprise value, reduce risk, strengthen the leadership team, or demonstrate that the company can support its next stage of investment.**

The advisory work therefore connects organizational diagnosis directly to value creation.

The central question becomes:

> **What must change in this company for the investment thesis or growth ambition to become operationally possible?**

That is the kind of question the practice is designed to answer.
#### Primary buyer

There are two primary buying paths for this practice.

### 1. Company Leadership

The most common direct buyer is the person with sufficient authority to act on a system-level diagnosis.

Typical buyers include:

- CEO
- President
- Founder
- occasionally a board chair or lead director

The strongest buyers are leaders who know the company must change but are not yet confident that the organization has correctly diagnosed what is holding it back.

They are often facing questions such as:

- Why is growth slowing?
- Why is strategy not translating into execution?
- Why is the organization becoming harder to operate as it scales?
- Why are product, customer, and commercial teams not moving together?
- What has to change before the next investment round, recapitalization, or growth phase?
- Which problems are structural versus leadership, process, product, or talent issues?

These buyers are not looking for generic consulting support.

They are looking for **clarity about what is actually happening and what decisions matter most now**.

The second buying path is through the investment firm.

Typical buyers include:

- PE partner or principal responsible for the investment

These buyers are focused less on one isolated organizational problem and more on whether a company can deliver against its **investment thesis or next-stage growth ambition**.

- the company needs to professionalize without becoming bureaucratic;
- customer or product performance is not supporting the growth thesis;

The value proposition for the investor is straightforward:

> **Determine what is truly constraining enterprise value before the company spends time, capital, or leadership energy fixing the wrong thing.**

Over time, this buying path is strategically important because a successful engagement can create **repeat access across a portfolio**, rather than producing only a single-company relationship.

 **Buying Trigger**

The strongest trigger is not simply that a company has a problem.

It is that the company has reached a **consequential inflection point** where leadership knows the current system is unlikely to support what needs to happen next.

That may be driven by:

- a new investment;
- pressure to improve growth or margin;
- preparation for additional funding;
- a stalled transformation;
- a major strategy shift;
- a new CEO or leadership team;
- acquisition integration;
- AI-driven business change;
- or a requirement to materially increase enterprise value.

In these situations, the buyer's core question is:

> **What has to change in this company for the next stage of value creation to become possible?**

### 6. The Flagship Offer

## Executive Diagnostic

A three- to four-week engagement designed to answer:

> What is actually happening here, why is it happening, and what decisions matter most now?

#### Phase 1 — Understand

Review:

strategy;

stated business priorities;

organizational design;

customer evidence;

operating metrics;

product strategy;

relevant research;

leadership assumptions;

decision structures;

incentives and success measures;

existing transformation initiatives.

Interview key people individually and, where useful, observe operating forums and decision-making behavior.

The goal is not consensus.

It is pattern recognition.

#### Phase 2 — Diagnose

Identify:

symptoms;

contradictions;

structural causes;

leadership behaviors;

incentive conflicts;

information gaps;

customer disconnects;

decision bottlenecks;

broken cross-functional seams;

areas where stated strategy and actual behavior diverge.

Develop and test competing hypotheses rather than prematurely settling on one explanation.

#### Phase 3 — Decide

Produce a concise executive diagnosis containing:

What we thought the problem was

What the evidence says

What the underlying system is producing

Where the steel thread is breaking

What should stop

What should change

The 3–5 decisions leadership now needs to make

What should happen during the next 90 days

The deliverable should be a decision instrument, not a giant consulting deck.

### 7. Follow-On Offer

## Executive Transformation Advisory

After the diagnostic, some clients will need help applying the conclusions.

This is advisory rather than operational ownership.

Possible cadence:

weekly or biweekly CEO/executive sessions;

leadership-team working sessions;

organizational design reviews;

decision reviews;

strategy critiques;

transformation checkpoints;

targeted research or sensemaking when new evidence emerges;

coaching individual executives where directly connected to the transformation.

The fundamental boundary:

> I help the organization think, decide, and course-correct. Management remains responsible for running it.

That boundary prevents the business from turning into fractional employment.

### 9. The URI Learning System

Maintain a private URI Diagnostic Journal throughout the engagement.

For every significant issue, capture seven things:

#### 1. Presented problem

What did people initially believe was happening?

#### 2. Observation and evidence

What did interviews, behavior, customer information, business data, artifacts, or organizational observation reveal?

#### 3. Patterns and contradictions

What repeated across sources? What did not fit the prevailing story? Where did behavior contradict stated intent?

#### 4. Diagnosis

What did I conclude was actually happening?

#### 5. Intervention

What question, framework, decision, reframing, or change did I introduce?

#### 6. Result

What happened afterward?

#### 7. Transferable principle

What did this teach me that might apply somewhere else?

Over time this creates the raw material for the advisory methodology.

The important unit of learning becomes:

Presented problem → Observation → Pattern → Diagnosis → Decision → Intervention → Outcome → Principle

### 14. Thought Leadership Strategy

The purpose of thought leadership is not to become a content creator.

It is to make my judgment visible before someone hires me.

Publish roughly one substantive idea every two to four weeks.

Write arguments such as:

Why organizations keep solving symptoms.

Why transformation problems are usually system problems.

Why horizontal visibility changes what leaders can see.

Why the seams between functions often matter more than the functions themselves.

What executives misunderstand about AI transformation.

Why organizational speed isn't primarily a process problem.

When autonomy works and when it creates chaos.

Why clarity matters more than alignment.

How leaders accidentally reward the opposite of their strategy.

What organizations lose when customer understanding becomes a department.

Why AI changes the role of leadership rather than eliminating it.

Why the best operating model depends on the problem being solved.

Why observation is an executive capability, not merely a research technique.

The important rule:

Start with what I believe, not what other experts have said.

Other thinkers can support the argument.

They should not be the argument.
