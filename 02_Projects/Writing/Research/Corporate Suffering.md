# Corporate Suffering: Research Summary

_Compiled to test an anecdotal pattern (LinkedIn posts, conversations) against data. Short answer: the anecdotes hold up, and manager-level suffering specifically is sharper than the workforce average._

---

## The Headline Numbers

**Global engagement is at a five-year low.** Gallup's _State of the Global Workplace: 2026 Report_ puts global engagement at 20%, down from 23% in 2022 and the lowest since 2020. Gallup ties this to roughly $10 trillion in lost productivity annually, about 9% of global GDP. U.S./Canada holds the highest regional engagement at 31%, which still means 69% of the American workforce is either not engaged or actively disengaged.

**Burnout specifically is climbing.** Eagle Hill Consulting's Workforce Burnout Survey 2025 (Ipsos, November 2025, 1,400 U.S. employees) found 55% of U.S. workers currently experiencing burnout, and 72% say it's actively diminishing their efficiency.

---

## Managers Are Cracking Harder Than Their Teams

This is the sharpest and most citable thread. Gallup's own reporting shows the engagement gap between managers and individual contributors has collapsed, from managers averaging +11 points above their teams in 2022 to just +3 in 2025. Managers are now barely more engaged than the people they lead, and Gallup attributes a meaningful share of this year's overall global decline directly to falling manager engagement.

**Caveat:** The widely-cited "managers account for 70% of the variance in team engagement" statistic gets attributed to the new report constantly, but it actually originates from Gallup's 2015 report, _State of the American Manager: Analytics and Advice for Leaders._ It's still Gallup, still legitimate, but it's a decade-old finding being recirculated as current data. Cite the correct year if used.

---

## AI Is Intensifying Work, Not Relieving It

This is the strongest, most current source. Berkeley Haas researchers Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye ran an eight-month study (April–December 2025) at a 200-person tech company, interviewing 40+ employees across engineering, product, design, research, and operations, published in _Harvard Business Review_.

> "We found that employees worked at a faster pace, took on a broader scope of tasks, and extended work into more hours of the day, often without being asked to do so." — Ranganathan & Ye

AI use wasn't mandated. The tools didn't free up time, they expanded what people felt capable of and willing to take on, and what started as self-driven momentum quietly became harder to sustain.

Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index (31,000 professionals, 31 countries, with LinkedIn) backs this from the leadership side: 53% of leaders say productivity must increase, while 80% of the global workforce reports lacking the time or energy to do their job, and employees are interrupted by a meeting, email, or ping every two minutes on average.

---

## The Newest Wrinkle: Cognitive Load, Not Just Volume

Multiple secondary sources (HRD Connect, Brad Hook, others) cite Deloitte's 2025 Workforce Intelligence Report finding that mental fatigue, cognitive strain, and decision friction have overtaken raw workload volume as the leading predictors of burnout, for the first time.

**Caveat:** No direct primary Deloitte report link was located, only secondhand citations of the same quoted line. The finding is consistent enough across sources to be likely real, but if this becomes a load-bearing stat in published work, track down the original Deloitte report before publication.

---

## Where This Leaves the Thesis

The data supports a specific version of the "corporate America is suffering" claim: it's not diffuse malaise, it's concentrated in the management layer, and AI is accelerating it rather than causing it. That's a tighter, more defensible frame than the general anecdote, and it lines up cleanly with the "AI is the deadline, not the cause" argument.
