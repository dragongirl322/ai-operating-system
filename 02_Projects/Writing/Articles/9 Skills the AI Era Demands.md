9 Skills the AI Era Demands

  

The design profession has spent the last decade arguing about the wrong things. Seat at the table. Reporting structures. Methodology debates. Meanwhile, AI walked in, sat down, and started doing the work.

Our job has always been simple: make sure technology works with and for humans, not the other way around. AI doesn’t change that job. It raises the stakes for doing it well.

When I say design, I mean the full discipline — research, content, data, and every craft focused on the human experience of technology.

This is not a piece about whether our profession is dying. It isn’t. But AI is building the products that billions of people will use to work, access care, and move through their lives. Someone has to make sure those products are built for humans. That has always been our job. The question is whether we show up equipped to do it.

If you lead teams, this is what to hire for, develop for, and hold your organization accountable to building. If you are an individual practitioner, this is your guide. These are the skills you must be building now. If it doesn’t light a fire in you, a career in tech may not have a place for you much longer.

These are not nice-to-have skills. They are the new non-negotiables for anyone who builds, leads, or hires for the human experience of technology. If your team is missing these capabilities, you are likely watching change happen around you rather than driving it. If you are an individual practitioner missing these capabilities, you are not growing, not advancing, and not delivering the value this moment demands. That is not a judgment. It is a reality check and an invitation to do something about it.

The Foundation: Mastery

The foundation that must exist to enable everything that follows is mastery. Not mastery as a destination but as a constant reach for something that lies just within your grasp. That framing, borrowed from Dr. Sarah Lewis in her book The Rise, is the only honest way to describe what this moment requires. Without that orientation, your work and your contributions are transactional at best. And transactional is not good enough right now. The work you do shapes the relationship humans have with technology. That demands more than competence. It demands a genuine, ongoing relationship with the work itself, one that pushes you further every time you think you’ve arrived.

Every skill on this list requires that posture. It is not a skill you develop alongside the nine. It is the condition that makes developing all nine possible.

  

1. AI Literacy and Augmented Practice

You cannot research about, design for, deliver content to, or measure what you do not understand. And right now, too many designers do not understand AI well enough to do any of those things responsibly.

The evidence is everywhere. Chat interfaces slapped onto existing products. Search interaction models applied to AI experiences. Designs that ignore the probabilistic nature of large language models, context windows, memory, and the role of evals in maintaining quality.

Trust is not a feature of AI experience. It is the threshold condition. If a human does not trust the system they are interacting with, no amount of capability, speed, or intelligence will make it valuable to them. Yuval Harari has warned extensively that AI systems designed without human understanding at their center risk not just poor experiences but the erosion of human agency itself. That is not hyperbole. That is the consequence of treating AI design as a technical problem rather than a human one. These are not minor oversights. They are fundamental failures that produce experiences that confuse, mislead, and erode the trust that every human-AI interaction depends on.

Understanding AI is not optional and it is not someone else’s job. Your engineers understand it because they are coding for it. Your product managers understand it because they must define requirements around it. If you are the only person in the room without a working knowledge of how these systems behave, you are the person adding the least value in the room. That is an untenable position for someone whose entire purpose is to advocate for the human on the other side.

The fastest way to build this literacy is to use the tools. Not occasionally. Regularly. Deliberately. You will learn how AI behaves by engaging with it, and you will discover ways to do your job faster and better in the process. Literacy and practice are not two separate pursuits. One builds the other.

  

2. Change Management and Resilience

We are living through arguably the most significant paradigm shift in the history of technology. Change is not coming. It is here, it is accelerating, and it is touching every role, every team, and every discipline in tech. If you work in technology and believe you can navigate this moment without intentional, deliberate change management, that is not optimism. It is naivety.

John Kotter’s principles of change management were not written for the AI moment but they apply to it with uncomfortable precision. Three of them deserve particular attention right now.

Create urgency. Leaders must name the moment clearly and honestly. The skills gap is real, the window to close it is narrowing, and the cost of waiting is compounding. Individuals must internalize that urgency rather than waiting to be convinced. The organizations that are moving are already moving. The ones that are waiting are already behind.

Enlist a volunteer army. Leaders should identify the people on their teams who are already building, already learning, already moving and empower them to bring others along. These are not the loudest people in the room. They are the ones doing the work quietly and getting better at it. Find them. Elevate them. Let them lead from where they are. Individuals who know they have gaps should be turning toward these people to model their behavior and learn from them.

Generate short-term wins. Leaders should be actively looking for early evidence that skill building is producing measurable impact on what the team is delivering — not just evidence of learning, but evidence of better work — and celebrating it loudly when they find it. Individuals should be setting themselves up to have an early win; something concrete and visible that demonstrates growth through real impact, not just effort.

None of this holds without sustained acceleration. The urgency that launches change is not the same as the discipline that sustains it. Leaders must continue to raise the bar while celebrating the progress that earns the right to raise it. Investing in growth, recognizing what is working, and making excellence feel achievable rather than punishing. Individuals must resist the comfort of early wins and keep reaching. That is mastery in practice. That is how change becomes culture rather than a moment.

  

3. Cross-Functional Influence

You are the expert on what humans need. That is not a soft claim. It is the foundation of your power in any cross-functional room. And it is the anchor that must come first.

Stephen Covey’s principle of seeking first to understand before seeking to be understood is not just good interpersonal advice. It is the operating model for cross-functional influence done well. Start with the human. Understand their needs, behaviors, and mental models deeply before you bring a single business requirement or technical constraint into the conversation. That understanding is what makes everything that follows credible.

Aristotle identified three modes of persuasion that have held up for over two thousand years: ethos, pathos, and logos. For experience professionals, they map with uncomfortable precision. Ethos is your human-centered anchor, the expertise and credibility you bring because you have done the work to understand the person on the other side. Pathos is your recognition of what the business needs and what the technology makes possible, meeting your partners where they are, understanding their goals, speaking their language. Logos is the data, the evidence that connects human need to business outcome in a way that is impossible to dismiss. Together they form a point of view that nobody else in the room could have built.

The risk that many fail to recognize is this: when you only do what others ask you to do, you are allowing business requirements and technical constraints to trump human needs. You are not influencing. You are executing. And execution without a human-centered point of view is exactly what AI tools now do faster and cheaper. Show up with the expertise you have earned. The humans on the other side are counting on it even if they don’t know your name.

  

4. Craft Mastery and Human-Centered Principles

The principles that govern great design have survived every major paradigm shift in the history of technology. From command line interfaces to graphical user interfaces. From local software to the internet. From desktop to mobile to wearables. From broadcast media to social networking. Through every one of those shifts, the fundamentals held: understand the human, honor their cognition, design with intention. Dieter Rams codified them. Jakob Nielsen operationalized them. And Steve Jobs reminded a generation of builders that “design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works.”

These principles are not nostalgic. They are the foundation that makes the next paradigm shift navigable. And AI is that shift. The practitioners who will lead through it are the ones who have internalized these principles deeply enough to apply them to interaction models, modalities, and human behaviors that have never existed before.

When a practitioner doesn’t apply sufficient rigor and intention to their craft, the discipline becomes a side dish. A nice to have. Something the team does when there is time and skips when there isn’t. That is not a position of strength and it is not a position that survives the current moment.

Researchers who deliver findings without actionable framing, ignoring the principles of human cognition and behavior that should shape both how they study and how they communicate what they learned. Designers who miss downstream interaction effects, ignore consistency, sidestep design systems, and produce work with no discernible attention to visual hierarchy, affordance, or cognitive load. Content that people have to work to understand. Measurement that tracks activity instead of outcomes. These are not minor gaps. They are the details that separate an expert from someone who is approximating expertise. And in a world where AI can approximate expertise quickly and cheaply, the only thing that separates a skilled practitioner from an algorithm is the craft and intention they bring to the work.

Craft mastery is not about perfection. It is about the relentless attention to the details that make the difference between work that is adequate and work that is genuinely good. It is also about understanding the humans on the other side well enough to know what good actually means for them — their cognitive load, their mental models, their need for clarity, accessibility, and trust. Human-centered principles are not a checklist. They are the foundation of the expertise that earns you a seat at the table and the credibility to hold it.

  

5. Systems Thinking

Humans don’t care about your org chart. They don’t experience your product through the lens of your vertical silos. They experience it as a whole, and when it feels fragmented, inconsistent, or incoherent, they don’t blame the team that owns that particular surface. They blame the product. They blame the brand.

Donella Meadows, in her foundational book Thinking in Systems, argued that hierarchical systems work best when they have strong bottom-up support. That is exactly what designers offer. The human-centered intelligence we generate is the bottom-up signal that should be informing decisions at every level above it. Without it, the system optimizes for the wrong things. And when designers can see the full system they can identify the bottlenecks, the places where humans get stuck and where no function is currently solving for their actual need. That is where the highest impact work lives.

The failure shows up in two ways: not recognizing the superpower at all, or over-investing in cross-functional alignment to the point where you follow where your partners take you rather than leading toward where the human needs you to go.

The further you advance in your career the more of the system you should be able to see and influence. But systems thinking is not reserved for senior practitioners. Even early career designers deliver better work when they recognize the adjacencies closest to their own and understand how a human moves between them.

This is a skill that is often ignored. It is also the most powerful. When experience professionals operate at the system level they become the steel thread that runs through every function’s success. Brand, product, engineering, sales, operations — their goals become your goals. Their success becomes your success. And the human on the other side becomes the thing every team cares about, not just the design team. That is not a collaboration style. That is a strategic posture. And it is the difference between a design organization that is tolerated and one that is indispensable.

  

6. Problem Framing and Strategic Judgment

People are going to experience your brand and your products regardless. The question is never whether those experiences happen. It is whether they are intentional. The difference between an experience that demonstrates a genuine understanding of human problems and behaviors and one that doesn’t is entirely determined by the quality of the discovery and thinking that happened before anyone started to design and build. Skipping discovery is not a shortcut. It is a gamble with the human on the other side.

Roger Martin, former dean of the Rotman School of Management and author of The Design of Business, argues that the quality of a solution is determined by the quality of the problem definition. Competitive advantage comes from framing challenges differently, seeing the underlying tensions, constraints, and trade-offs that others overlook. Strategy, and by extension design, is fundamentally about choosing the right problem to solve before choosing how to solve it. Peter Drucker, widely regarded as the father of modern management, made the same point from a different angle: “There is nothing so useless as doing efficiently that which should not be done at all.” Solving the wrong problem, no matter how skillfully, creates waste and misalignment. But choosing the right problem is only half of it. The other half is building the solution in a way that matches how people think, feel, and behave. That is where discipline-specific expertise becomes the differentiator.

The practitioners who are exceptional at this skill start with the business vision and strategy, use that to frame the human problem clearly, and then apply their craft to solve it in a way that is grounded in human cognition and behavior. They are not asking how do I study this or how do I design this. They are asking what does this human need, what does this business need, and what does the technology make possible. Then they bring their expertise to bear on the answer. That is strategic judgment. And it is what separates design leadership from design execution.

  

7. Product and Business Acumen

It is difficult to deliver business value if you don’t understand the business. It is difficult to design product experiences if you don’t understand the product. These are not radical statements. They are obvious ones. And yet the design profession has a long history of producing work that is disconnected from both.

Peter Drucker argued that the purpose of business is to create a customer. Not to build a product. Not to generate revenue. To create a customer. That framing should resonate deeply with experience professionals because it means that understanding the business is not a concession to corporate priorities. It is the most human centered thing a practitioner can do. When you understand how the business creates value, you understand what the human on the other side is being offered. And when you understand that, you can design, research, write, and measure in ways that make that value real and accessible to them.

When this skill is absent the work becomes self-referential. Research conducted for the sake of research. Design produced for the sake of design. Content written for the sake of writing. Measurement tracked for the sake of tracking. None of it tethered to what the business needs to achieve or what the product needs to deliver. Over time that disconnect is not invisible. It accumulates. And the practitioner who cannot connect their work to business outcomes stops being an asset and becomes a liability.

When this skill is present it looks like a practitioner who reads what their business leaders read, understands how the company makes money, knows the product strategy, and uses that knowledge to make sharper decisions about what to study, what to design, what to say, and what to measure. They don’t wait to be handed context. They go get it. And when they show up in the room they are speaking the language of the people who make decisions, not asking those people to learn theirs.

  

8. Data Literacy

Design disciplines are among the best at data literacy. Researchers and data scientists are built on it. Designers tend to apply it naturally to problem solving. This is a strength of the discipline, and it deserves to be treated as one, not taken for granted and not underutilized. Where it breaks down is not in the having of it. It is in the application of it.

In fast moving organizations, the demand for decisions outpaces the appetite for full rigor. Experience professionals who struggle to make rigor trade-offs in favor of strategic velocity can freeze when there isn’t time to do things completely. But freezing is not neutral. When experience professionals step back from the data conversation because conditions aren’t perfect, the work doesn’t stop. It continues without data, or with data interpreted by people who lack the literacy to do it well. The practitioners who excel here have learned to apply grounded theory thinking: use what is known in the moment, make the best decision the evidence supports, and trust that the iterative nature of innovation creates space to build on that foundation later. Sufficient rigor in the right moment is not a compromise. It is a judgment call. And it is a skill.

The other place it breaks down is influence. Data literacy without the willingness to use it as a lever is a wasted advantage. Too many experience professionals sit on insights that should be shaping decisions and framing problems and instead wait to be asked. The practitioners who are exceptional at this skill don’t follow where their cross-functional partners point them. They use what they know to reframe the problem, redirect the conversation, and lead toward the work that actually matters.

Brené Brown has said that stories are just data with a soul. That framing captures something that the best experience professionals understand intuitively: data without the human context that gives it meaning is just numbers, and human context without the data to ground it is just intuition. The practitioners who are exceptional at data literacy hold both simultaneously. They know when the evidence is sufficient to act and when it needs to go deeper. They know how to translate what the data shows into a story that moves people. And they know that the willingness to do both is what makes data literacy a leadership capability rather than a technical one.

Data literacy is a differentiator. By embracing it and making ourselves accountable for influencing through it, we are much better able to serve the human on the other side of technology.

  

9. Storytelling and Executive Communication

The work is not enough. It has never been enough. A research finding that stays in a deck, a design that never gets championed, a measurement that no one acts on — none of it moves anything. What moves organizations is the story told about the work and the clarity with which it connects to what the organization is trying to achieve.

Experience professionals have a tendency to get focused on the execution of the work rather than the outcome the work is intended to serve. And there is sometimes an assumption — rarely stated but often present — that the quality of the work will speak for itself. It won’t. Not in complex organizations. Not at the pace that AI is forcing decisions to be made. Not across functions that are already moving fast in their own directions.

The experience professionals who master this skill build something more than communication capability. They build scaffolding. Scaffolding for how the organization prioritizes, because a compelling, evidence-backed story about human need is the most powerful tool for getting the right problems to the top of the list. Scaffolding for how the organization works and builds, because when teams understand the human story behind what they are building, they make better decisions at every level of execution. And scaffolding for how the organization sells, because when the human story is told well internally, it doesn’t stay internal. It becomes the story marketers tell, the story sales teams tell, and ultimately the story customers use to understand the value of what has been built for them. That is the reach of great storytelling. It doesn’t require the designer to be in the room. It requires the story to be good enough that everyone who is in the room can tell it.

Brené Brown writes in Dare to Lead that the most dangerous stories we make up are the ones we tell ourselves when we don’t have enough information. The same is true in organizations. When experience professionals don’t tell the story of what they know and what it means, everyone else fills the void with their own version. The product team tells a features story. The sales team tells a pricing story. The marketing team tells a brand story. And the human on the other side experiences the gap between all three. Great storytelling is not just communication. It is the act of making sure the human story is the one that travels.

This is also how experience stops being a department and starts being a capability. When experience professionals tell compelling stories about what humans need and why it matters, they create the conditions for the entire organization to become design obsessed. Not just the design team. Everyone. That is the highest order impact a design professional can have, and it starts with the story.

  

Most humans don’t wake up thinking about the technology they are going to use today. They wake up wanting to live fulfilling, purposeful lives. Technology plays a profound role in whether that is possible, and AI is the most powerful technology in the history of humanity. It has the potential to help people live and work in ways we are only beginning to imagine. But only if it is designed and built with humans in mind.

That is not a burden. It is the most important and interesting work in the world. And it has never been more available to the people who show up equipped to do it.

These nine skills are not a wish list. They are the new baseline. For leaders, they are the standard to hire for, develop for, and hold your organizations accountable to. For individuals, they are the map and the guide. Where you are strong, go deeper. Where you have gaps, close them. Not because someone asked you to. Because the moment demands it and the human on the other side deserves it.

  

Tammy Snow is a design executive who builds the organizational and human conditions for technology to deliver on its promise. She is driven by a single standard: every person who experiences what we build should find themselves more capable, more confident, and more free.
