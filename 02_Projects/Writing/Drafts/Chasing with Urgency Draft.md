I spent most of my life involved in one sport or another yet I could never embrace running. It always felt so tedious and arduous to me but I did it frequently as a "should" to keep myself fit and lean. That all changed for me nearly 20 years ago when I decided that running would be a good hobby for me and set a goal of running a half marathon. I learned that running not only made me feel great physically but it unlocked a level of creativity and zest for life I didn't know I had in me. I got hooked. One of the things I loved about running is that I knew it would be hard, especially the first mile as my body acclimated to the physical stress, but I also knew exactly what the goal was and why and consistently felt the reward at the end of a long run. 

I've been in tech for almost 40 years and have spent nearly 20 years as a people leader and the past 10 at the executive leadership level. Throughout those years I have mostly loved the work and have mostly understood exactly what I was working toward and why. For the past year, I've been chasing something that was never entirely clear to me with a sense of urgency that was unrelenting. Some of that was created by the uncertainty the emergence of AI has created. Some of that was created by the ever changing landscape of the company I work for. Ultimately, it was all created by me and my desire to always be achieving, proving to myself that I'm good enough and maybe even great once in a while. 

I recently stepped away from a role I've been in for nearly 5 years. I have loved the work and thoroughly enjoyed leading the team and collaborating with cross functional partners. In most ways, I was ready to leave and step into a period of rest, reconnection, and creativity without any plans for what comes next. At the same time, I knew that after several years of chasing achievement and the past five years of traveling at least once a month, working 10 - 12 hour days, leading a team of ~300, that I would need time to unwind and detox. 

Research shows us that neurologically, the modern digital world creates a **dopamine-cortisol loop**: notifications create anticipatory dopamine spikes that compel checking → checking reveals demands that trigger cortisol → completing the demand provides momentary relief → the cycle resets. Over time, the brain structures responsible for _calm, sustained focus_ are literally under-exercised and may atrophy, while the stress-response pathways become chronically activated. 

Because I am who I am, I left prepared to focus on a detox and rewiring myself to embrace calm, slow, steady. It turns out, this is much harder than I expected. I consider myself to be quite self-aware and highly disciplined. I had spent the past year consistently engaging in a few key non-negotiables: at least 7 hours of sleep each night, at least three 15 minute walks per day, no work email or slack until after my first coffee, no work past 9 pm. While all of those habits have helped me get through the past year of constant churn, stress, and urgency, I'm finding it difficult to truly disengage from wanting to plan and start chasing new ways to achieve. I'm sure this isn't unique to just me and that others have, are, or will experience similar.

I love things that come in three (science tells us there is power in 3) so here are three things I'm doing to support myself in a clear detox period:

1. I'm seeking out non-digital activities that help me feel full and devoting more time to those things than being on my devices. For example, reading, time with my family playing games, trips to the store with my wife, phone conversations with friends. 
2. Journaling. I've kept a daily journal in the mornings for over a year and have recently added a nightly "brag" journal that I use to help me provide myself positive affirmations.
3. I'm taking time during the day to do absolutely nothing. To just sit and think, often in the sunshine because I love the feeling of the sun on my face. 

I'm less than a week in and am glad I've got three things to help ground me. At the same time, I struggle daily not to give in to that need to re-engage, to get chasing something again.

I know from my experience with running that the next chapter will be great if I give myself space to grieve the loss of doing work I loved with people I care for and settling into life so creativity can flow. My knees forced me to stop running over 10 years ago due to a knee surgery followed by a blood clot and tearing the meniscus in my other knee. I decided another surgery wasn't in my best interest and gave up running. I have now shifted to walking and strength training and have loved the neurological, physical, and social benefits it brings. While the loss of running was initially hard, I can now look back with pride in what I accomplished and feel great about the way I move my body now. I am confident I'll say the same thing professionally in the weeks and months to come.
