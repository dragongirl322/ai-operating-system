In early September, 1996, I told my girlfriend, "I will never work for Bill Gates." I was full of conviction. I remember it so well. We were at a stop sign right in front of the house I grew up in. I don't remember how the topic of Bill Gates came up. At the time, I worked for Novell, the leader in networking software. About 10 days after that bold proclamation, I got an email from a former Novell colleague who had taken a management role at Microsoft inviting me to consider a role he had on his team. We agreed to speak and he told me a little about the role and I said "Nope. I'm not going to do it." His response was to at least get the free trip and to appeal to my love of good coffee and microbrew beers. That worked. I spent two days in late September interviewing and left hoping I'd get the offer. The people I met with seemed great. The weather in Seattle that time of year was stunning. It felt like a place I could thrive. I started working at Microsoft on November 4, 1996 and spent 11 years there. 

I loved my time at Microsoft. It felt like graduate school to me and that matters because I'd dropped out of college to start working in tech in 1988. I learned so many great things in my time there. It is where I found my own point of view as a researcher and learned how to stand by it with courage and conviction. I first became a people leader at Microsoft and learned how to performance manage, calibrate, and develop talent. I learned that lesbians wear comfortable shoes and realized I should have known because I am, after all, a lesbian. I was able to be fully out, a luxury I didn't believe I could afford while working in Utah for Novell. 

I left Microsoft in 2008 when my son was almost four. I realized I was missing out on so many moments I wanted to be a part of so I started a consultancy. I spent four years working independently and in late 2011 told my wife "I'll never work in corporate again." I was, once again, full of conviction. In January of 2012, I started a contract role with Expedia Group. It was the first time in four years that I was set to spent the majority of my time in an office working beside other people. I remember standing at the elevator bank on the 12th floor of the Expedia tower in Bellevue with a badge on my hip and wondering what the fuck I'd signed up for. A few months later I realized I was liking the work, I liked the people, and wearing that badge wasn't so awful. I accepted a full-time role in April of 2012.

In June of 2012, I told my wife "I'll never manage people again." Yes, I was full of conviction this time, too. Not too long after that I heard through the grapevine that my boss was planning on hiring a manager of research. I didn't like that idea at all because I liked my boss a lot and didn't want to report to anyone else. I realized I had a choice to make: either I go back on my "I'll never" or I report to someone I might not respect. In my next 1:1 with my boss, Bill, our conversation went something like this: "I hear you're planning to hire a research manager?" "I am. I have too many direct reports." "well, you can throw my name in the hat." "I was hoping you'd say that! I didn't want to ask you because you said you didn't want to manage again." "Yeah, I don't but I'd rather have the manager be the devil I know, me, than somebody I don't like." Within a couple of weeks, I was managing the team. I loved my time at Expedia. It is where I learned how to lead, not just manage. I learned about the world of test and learn. I learned about two sided marketplaces and platform dynamics. I learned how to be truly myself in a work environment and to allow myself to care about and be cared for by other people. I worked with some of the most amazing leaders and had an opportunity to report directly to the executive leader I've admired most in my long career and learned from him about riding the tiger, a habit I've carried with me since. 

You might be wondering if I learned from all of this to never say never. I doubt it. But I did learn two important things:

1. We humans really are horrible at predicting what our future behavior will be. As a researcher I've always *thought* this was true and I learned first hand that it is.
2. It is a good thing to remain open to possibilities we haven't envisioned for ourselves. Sometimes the thing we didn't think we wanted ends up being exactly what we needed.
