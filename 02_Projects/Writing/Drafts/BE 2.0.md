It was spring of 2020 and I was working from home and, like everybody else, stuck at home with my thoughts when I wasn't working. My wife was in the middle of chemo treatment for stage IV cancer. To keep myself grounded and occupied, I'd sit at my kitchen table coloring while listening to Brene Brown's *Dare to Lead* podcast through my earbuds. One of my favorite episodes was her interview with Jim Collins, author of several great leadership books including "Good to Great." I was immediately drawn to his ideas because, like Brene, he is a grounded theory researcher and as a researcher myself, I could relate to the conversation. They talked about creating a conceptual vessel for insights, *The Stockdale Paradox*, leadership, and his book, BE 2.0, a new and improved version of a book he and Bill Lazier had written many years previously. I immediately ordered the book and read it shortly after it arrived. It is one of the best leadership books I've read and definitely the best in terms of understanding business context. The top three things concepts I took away from the book are the hedgehog concept, level 5 leadership, and to never stifle a generous impulse.

**The hedgehog concept** is the idea that the kind of focus that moves a business to greatness only shows up where three things overlap: What you are deeply passionate about, what you can genuinely be the best in the world at, and what drives your economic engine. Collins borrows the name from an old Isaiah Berlin essay about foxes and hedgehogs. The fox knows a lot of things and chases all of them. The hedgehog knows one thing and does it, over and over, no matter what comes at it.  When I read about this concept in BE 2.0, I realized that I've spent most of my life as a fox. I'm curious about most things, decent at a lot of things, and rarely willing to put anything aside long enough to find that one thing I'm most passionate about. Learning about the concept helped me to see the importance of becoming more focused in my professional life, particularly as a leader of a large organization. It helped me get more disciplined and focused and better able to say "no" to things that fell outside of the three circles. 

**Level 5 leadership** is the top of Collins's five-level leadership hierarchy: Level 5 leaders build enduring greatness through a paradoxical blend of personal humility and professional will.  The Level 5 leader wants greatness the same way anyone with drive wants it, they just want it for the cause, the company, the mission, more than they want it for themselves. They'll make the brutal call and hand the credit to someone else without blinking, because none of this was ever about being seen. I just love that. Collins doesn't ask leaders to shrink. He asks them to point all that will somewhere other than their own reflection.

The book starts with a list of principles Jim learned from his mentor and father figure, Bill Lazier. The entire list is fantastic and my favorite is to **never stifle a generous impulse**. Jim shares the story of blah, and blah. I've come to appreciate how completely good it feels to be generous with my time, experiences, and expertise. 

I highly recommend this book. It is the best for understanding business principles and frameworks, from the importance of catalyzing a vision and strategy to becoming a better leader. The book is full of several stories that make it easy to understand and learn how to apply concepts and are a great illustration of how Jim Collins creates a conceptual vessel. Perhaps my favorite thing about the book is how full of humanity it is, especially the way Jim Collins speaks about his relationship with Bill Lazier.
