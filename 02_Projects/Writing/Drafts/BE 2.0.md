It was spring of 2020 and I was working from home and, like everybody else, stuck at home with my thoughts when I wasn't working. My wife was in the middle of chemo treatment for stage IV cancer. To keep myself grounded and occupied, I'd sit at my kitchen table coloring while listening to Brené Brown's *Dare to Lead* podcast through my earbuds.

One of my favorite episodes was her interview with Jim Collins, author of several great leadership books including "Good to Great." I was immediately drawn to his ideas because, like Brené, he is a grounded theory researcher and as a researcher myself, I could relate to the conversation. They talked about creating a conceptual vessel for insights, *The Stockdale Paradox*, leadership, and his book, BE 2.0, a new and improved version of a book he and Bill Lazier had written many years previously. I immediately ordered the book and read it shortly after it arrived. It is one of the best leadership books I've read and definitely the best in terms of understanding business context.

The top lessons I took away from the book are the hedgehog concept, level 5 leadership, the Stockdale Paradox, and to never stifle a generous impulse.

**The hedgehog concept** is the idea that the kind of focus that moves a business to greatness only shows up where three things overlap: What you are deeply passionate about, what you can genuinely be the best in the world at, and what drives your economic engine. Collins borrows the name from an old Isaiah Berlin essay about foxes and hedgehogs. The fox knows a lot of things and chases all of them. The hedgehog knows one thing and does it, over and over, no matter what comes at it. When I read about this concept in BE 2.0, I realized that I've spent most of my life as a fox. I'm curious about most things, decent at a lot of things, and rarely willing to put anything aside long enough to find that one thing I'm most passionate about. Learning about the concept helped me to see the importance of becoming more focused in my professional life, particularly as a leader of a large organization. It helped me get better at saying "no" to things that fell outside of the three circles.

**Level 5 leadership** is the top of Collins's five-level leadership hierarchy: Level 5 leaders build enduring greatness through a paradoxical blend of personal humility and professional will. The Level 5 leader wants greatness the same way anyone with drive wants it. They just want it for the cause, the company, the mission, more than they want it for themselves. They'll make the brutal call and hand the credit to someone else without blinking, because their ambition isn't about being seen but about getting shit done. I just love that. Collins doesn't ask leaders to shrink. He asks them to point all that will somewhere other than their own reflection.

Jim Stockdale was the highest-ranking U.S. military officer held as a POW during the Vietnam War. Collins interviewed him for his book *Good to Great* and learned that the prisoners who didn't make it out were the optimists. They would tell themselves "we'll be out by Christmas," and when Christmas came and went, they told themselves "we'll be out by Easter," and so on. Each failed deadline crushed them a little more until it broke them. The **Stockdale Paradox** is that you must retain unwavering faith that you will prevail in the end, while simultaneously confronting the most brutal facts of your current reality, no matter what they are. Since learning about this, I've found that we are often in situations where the ability to accept reality while remaining determined and hopeful is critical.

The book starts with a list of principles Jim learned from his mentor and father figure, Bill Lazier. The entire list is fantastic and my favorite is to **never stifle a generous impulse**. Jim tells a story about two enormous wooden crates that showed up on his porch one day, shipped by Bill, packed with dozens of bottles of wine from France, Italy, and California. There was no special occasion for the gift and it wasn't something Jim had asked for. It was just Bill being Bill. The principle itself actually came from Bill's own mentor, HP co-founder William Hewlett, but Bill lived it in a way that made it stick. Collins writes that because Bill gave so much away, it kept coming back to him, and that return only made him more generous, not less. I recognize that loop. Every time I've acted on a generous impulse instead of talking myself out of it, something has come back to me that I didn't expect and couldn't have planned for.

Of all of the leadership books I've read, this one is the most practical and actionable. It is one I keep returning to over and over again. Perhaps my favorite thing about the book is how full of humanity it is, especially the way Jim Collins speaks about his relationship with Bill Lazier.
