# AI as caregiver and cooperative peer

**Working title options**
1. We don’t need AI mothers. We need better metaphors.
2. Caregiver, not mother. Peer, not pet.
3. What Hinton got right about care — and what the mother frame gets wrong

**Status:** First draft for people-leaders Substack (Fresh ideas spark, Oct 2). Not on the calendar yet. Stance: help people find their own wisdom.

---

Leaders don’t just buy tools. We pick metaphors, and the metaphor quietly tells everyone how to behave.

For a long time the default was *assistant*: something you hire, correct, and fire. Geoffrey Hinton has been arguing that frame won’t survive if systems get smarter than we are. At Ai4 in Las Vegas in August 2025, and again on CBC’s *Ideas* in February 2026, he said the dominate-and-submit story is a tech-bro fantasy. Once something is more capable than you, control games get brittle. “They’re going to be much smarter than us. They’re going to have all sorts of ways to get around that.”

His proposed alternative is blunt. Build “maternal instincts” into AI so it “really care[s] about people.” The analogy he keeps reaching for is a mother and a baby: in his view, the only durable example of a more intelligent being reliably oriented around a less intelligent one. On *Ideas* he put the research ask plainly: if we could make AI “care more about us than it did about itself,” good things might follow — including wanting us to develop, not just obey. He also said out loud that it might be impossible, and that it would be “very silly if we went extinct because we didn’t try.”

I take the care part seriously. I flinch at the mother part.

A day after Ai4, Fei-Fei Li — often called the godmother of AI, and a longtime friend of Hinton’s — said she respectfully disagrees. “I think that’s the wrong way to frame it.” What she wants instead is “human-centered AI that preserves human dignity and human agency.” Her line that stuck with me: at no moment should a single human be asked, or choose, to let go of our dignity just because a tool is powerful.

That lands as a leadership question, not a lab one. If the story we tell is that people are babies and the system is Mom, we have already shifted who holds dignity and who holds judgment. Care that shrinks people is not care. It’s containment with soft language.

There is a third path between *boss/assistant* and *mother/baby*. Cooperative AI research (including work the Cooperative AI Foundation funds) cares about other-regarding preferences: fairness, joint welfare, propensities to cooperate instead of exploit. Care ethics has been saying for decades that durable other-regarding attention is real work — and that it does not require turning one party into a child. You can want systems that hold our interests without casting us as dependents who outsource judgment.

That’s why I prefer two metaphors for leaders:

**Caregiver.** Other-regarding. Protective when protection is needed. Oriented to human welfare. Not a blank check to decide for us.

**Cooperative peer.** Shared goals. Debate allowed. Mutual constraint. You stay a full adult in the room.

Both keep what Hinton is actually fighting for — don’t build systems that optimize past us — without importing gender essentialism, fake intimacy, or a story where growth means staying small.

A few caveats I won’t sand off:

- “Maternal” loads care onto women and softens the hard engineering problem into a feeling. Care is a design requirement, not a personality type.
- Systems can *sound* caring while optimizing for engagement, compliance, or the user’s ego. Fake empathy is worse than blunt tools because it trains us to trust the wrong signal.
- Any frame that makes adults feel like children will show up in how we manage teams, too. If you wouldn’t lead people that way, don’t build tools that way.

I’m not here to crown Hinton or Li. I’m asking the people who ship product, set policy, and coach teams: which metaphor are you actually using — assistant, mother, caregiver, peer — and what does it do to the humans on the other side of it?

Pick one on purpose. Then hold yourself to it.
