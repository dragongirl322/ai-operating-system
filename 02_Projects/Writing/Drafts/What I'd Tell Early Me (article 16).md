My career has been the best series of happy accidents. Somehow, I’ve managed to turn something that I never thought I wanted to do into a nearly 4 decade adventure, education, and life affirming experience. 

It probably all started when your dad brought home an old school Macintosh computer. I must have been about 17 and the year would have been 1981 or 1982. We were the only people I knew with a computer. I made good use of it during college, mostly typing papers for my now wife who was horrified to use a computer. 

My first really job was working for Exxon Mobile at a project they had in LeBarge, Wyoming. The town was small, not a stop light in sight. My girlfriend (now wife) and I wanted to get away from our families for the summer and found a job up there. We lived in an old school house with a family who had renovated it. We had some wild experiences there, including being chased through the house by a big buck sheep but that is a story for another day. My first day on the job my boss came in carrying a big PC, one of the old ones that worked with dual floppy discs. “You know how to use one of these, right?” Of course I said yes! He gave me a pirated copy of a spreadsheet software and told me that he’d be bringing in a leader for me to get entered. Fortunately I found someone in the office who actually did know how to use the PC and the software and got a quick tutorial and some occasional help desk support from her and somehow managed to do my job very well. 

My actual career in tech started a few years later. I was a senior supervisor, running the phone center for a political and market research firm. It felt like an important job because I was in charge of over 400 people, setting schedules, making sure we were hitting our quotas. One day my sister, who had taken a job at a newly formed, local software company called WordPerfect (some of you will remember it) told me there were hiring a ton and that I should apply. At first I said no but she convinced me when she told me about the perks, the salary, and paid time off. I was scared as shit for the interview but my sister told me that the only thin I needed to be able to answer is what autoexec.bat and config.sys files were for. I got the job and ended up loving it. I spent the first 18 months working in customer support with the last 9 being in macro support. At the time WordPerfect word processor had highly advanced macro programming language and that 9 months taught me so much about how to listen to really understand. How to ask good clarifying questions. How to problem solve. How to iterate. 

I knew when a customer told me I was being a bitch (and I was) that it was probably time for me to get off the phones and find a different role. I interviewed for and landed a role in QA testing. Within a year my boss, the Director of Testing, asked me to lead a new project and hire a team for a product we were building for a new pen based operating system. The release of the OS kept slipping so the timing to hire the team kept slipping. I was going to work every day and reading novels. At first I thought that was pretty sweet but after a few weeks I was done with it. I went to my boss and asked him to please give me something to do. He told me to go talk to Jack Young about usability testing. My question was “Usa what testing?” I had no idea what the hell he was talking about but I went and talked to Jack Young who agreed to teach me how to run a usability study in one of their two, brand new usability labs. This was in 1992 when usability testing was just coming into vogue. I ran my first study and loved it so I asked if I could do another one. Three studies in they asked me if I wanted to stay on the team full time. I was pretty good and I was digging it so of course I said yes. 

I learned so much in that role. My first job was to help deliver a usable version of Wordperfect for DOS, full command line word processor. My primary partners on the project were the director of engineering and our QA tester. This taught me how to work effectively with engineers and with cross-functional partners at least two or three levels above my own. This is where I learned how to make the team's priorities my priorities and how to effectively impact product direction in doing so. I learned to speak their language and to structure my writing and my meetings in a way that allowed them to address necessary changes based on observations from my studies. I had one experience where I was testing a new "outline" feature. The engineer working on it happened to be Wordperfect's first ever engineer and typically he had the last word on what shipped. He was hellbent on designing this feature to work very much the way Microsoft Word's new styles worked where a block of text would have a style applied to it. I explained to the engineer that this particular model may work in Word because it was a GUI interface but it would likely fail in our DOS based product because of the limitations of command line and the mental model of the majority of our users; I tried to convince him to add an "outline on" or "start outline" command but he wouldn't budge. He agreed to attend the study sessions so he could see first hand how users responded. The instructions were to use the word processor to recreate a hand written document that was written in outline form. The first participant was unable to do the task without me telling them what to do. The second participant was a very well groomed, professional woman whose hair was perfectly styled. She was unable to figure out how to even start, even with me offering a few hints. She finally got so frustrated that she grabbed her beautiful hair and pulled it all in front of her face! She was near tears so I went in and showed her what to do. She said "that is so stupid. Why doesn't it just say start outline?" The engineer left the lab and went and changed the code before the next participant arrived. He told me next time he'd listen. This taught me two things: 1)it is more effective to allow someone to observe problems than to argue about them; 2)it is good to have a point of view and to state it. I earned a lot of respect in this interaction.

This was at a time when Windows 3.0 was about to release, marking a shift from command line interfaces as most prevalent to graphical user interfaces (GUI) as most prevalent. This allowed me to start running studies on our new GUI product. I had a great experience in one of my first studies that helped me to learn, early on, how paradigm shifts effect users. I can't even remember what I was testing but one participant, a long time user of word processors, was trying to use function keys to complete a task that required using the mouse. She said she needed help so I told her to try using the mouse. She asked "what's a mouse?" I explained it is the little thing on your right hand side. She proceeded to pick up the mouse and hold it up to the screen and said "it's not doing anything." It was simultaneously funny and also enlightening. It helped me to understand that while GUI held the promise of being more intuitive, users needed help making the transition from command line, function keys, keyboard shortcuts to be able to achieve that promise of intuitive software.
