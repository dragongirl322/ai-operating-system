A little over 3 years ago, I wrote a set of four rules on the whiteboard that sits in my mud room. I look at it several times a day and wanted these rules where I'd be constantly reminded. The rules:

Good sleep
Good food
Move & get fresh air
Lower stress

We were just coming out of covid. Throughout 2020, my wife had gone through chemo, radiation, and surgeries for stage 4 cancer. My nineteen-year-old autistic son was figuring out how to navigate a post-covid world. I had just taken on a leadership role with huge scope and a large, multi-discipline team. I loved the work enough that I was putting in significant hours every day and rarely taking time off. My body was telling me that my central nervous system was on overload. These are the four non-negotiable rules I set for myself.

This is a tough time to be a people leader in tech. You are dealing with your own stress and fear, and you are carrying responsibility for your team: guiding them, supporting them, and trying to protect them from chaos. It is a lot. If you want to be a reliable people leader, you have to manage yourself first: body, mind, and heart.

The data on this is clear and compelling. Gallup's *State of the Global Workplace: 2026* report finds global engagement at 20% in 2025, the lowest since 2020, tied to roughly $10 trillion in lost productivity. Manager engagement has dropped nine points since 2022, including a five-point drop in a single year, from 27% to 22%. The old "manager premium" is basically gone: managers sit at 22%, individual contributors at 19%. Gallup ties most of the overall decline to that collapse. The same report says leaders, compared with individual contributors, are more likely to have felt a lot of stress, anger, sadness, and loneliness the day before. You cannot be a steady leader if you are running on empty.

I saw this up close and personal a few years ago. I was tired and grieving my 27-year-old nephew, who had passed away a few weeks earlier. I had a particularly stressful commute. One of my direct reports came to me with a problem they characterized as urgent. Rather than taking a couple of minutes to acknowledge the urgency and get a high-level idea of what was happening, I responded dismissively and with more curtness than I normally would. A few hours later I reflected on the interaction and realized I hadn't shown up in the way I needed to. I approached the individual and apologized. I learned that my dismissiveness led to them feeling brushed off and stressed over a situation I could have helped them resolve in a couple of minutes. Sadly, I had caused them to question my confidence in them. On top of that, I had been uncharacteristically impatient with my son over the past couple of weeks. I realized I had been totally neglecting myself, skipping meals, staying up late, and not exercising regularly. This is what prompted me to write on the whiteboard.

This story illustrates how poor self-management bleeds into how you manage your team. Christopher Barnes and colleagues followed supervisors and their teams across ten workdays. Poor sleep *quality* — not just hours in bed — predicted leader ego depletion that day, which predicted more abusive supervision, which predicted lower engagement on the team. Your body is not a private matter. The people who report to you feel it whether you name it or not.

In a 2005 study, Thomas Sy, Stéphane Côté, and Richard Saavedra showed that a leader's mood is contagious: when the leader is in a positive mood, the group coordinates better; when the leader is in a negative mood, the group works harder and coordinates worse. Sigal Barsade's work on emotional contagion in groups found the same pattern: emotion moves, and it changes cooperation and conflict. This is why "I'll rest when things calm down" is a leadership problem, not a calendar problem. Things do not calm down. Your nervous system is the climate.

A question I frequently get is "how are you able to stay so calm and steady?" It is not a temperament. I am intentional and consistent about managing myself. I first took this seriously when I read Stephen Covey's *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* and M. Scott Peck's *The Road Less Traveled*. Covey called it sharpening the saw. Peck called it discipline: life is difficult, and the way through is not mood, it is practice. I treated both as good ideas for a long time. The whiteboard is where they stopped being ideas. Sleep, food, movement, and less stress, every day, especially when the job is loud. That is how I stay usable to the people I lead.
