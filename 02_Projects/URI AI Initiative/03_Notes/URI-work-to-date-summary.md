## What we have learned about the business

URI is a long-standing user research firm under pressure to become a clearer, more saleable growth company. Trinh cares deeply about a strong outcome in roughly the next 18 to 24 months. The firm needs transformation, not cosmetic AI projects.

**Clients and concentration.** Clients want faster, more strategic, actionable insight and thought leadership, not only research execution. Google is the largest client. Amazon is thin and price-sensitive. Heavy reliance on too few clients is a real risk. Jeff separately named client diversification as a challenge.

**Org and accountability.** Research is the profitable core. Sales and marketing capability is widely seen as weak. Not enough people are accountable for selling. Innovation (including Panel Pro and Dalien’s AI work) has sat under Justin with low transparency to Trinh and the rest of the company. Sales is moving to Shawn (targeted for November 1, alongside FY27 strategy rollout). Ken, Brooke, Shawn, and Jeff converge on the same themes: unclear accountability, lost focus, and a vision that is not concrete enough for people to work toward. Trinh herself called the vision too flowery.

**CEO authority and founder transition.** Dan named Trinh CEO because he trusts and respects her and has told Tam he would support Trinh asking Justin to leave if she judged that necessary. Trinh rejected Justin’s proposal that they serve as co-CEOs because she believes two-headed leadership will not work. The broader team sees Trinh—not Justin—as the leader and wants her to lean further into the role. The unresolved issue is not whether she has legitimacy, capability, or owner backing; it is whether she will consistently enforce CEO authority when doing so creates conflict with Justin. Justin is reported to bypass her, and she knows this occurs but has not consistently confronted it.

**Working diagnosis — early and testable.** URI is experiencing an incomplete founder-to-CEO operating transition. Formal authority, investor backing, team legitimacy, and CEO capability are present. The central constraint is inconsistent enforcement of CEO authority where decisions affect Justin’s identity, status, or historic influence. Trinh has described the situation as hard: she feels gratitude and personal obligation to Justin, does not want to undermine his self-esteem as founder, acknowledges that her own lack of action is a problem, and has asked Tam to hold her accountable. This dynamic appears to reinforce fragmented ownership, hidden work, and weak accountability. The diagnosis will be tested through the AI pivot, the dated disposition of the existing HITL initiative, visible owner endorsement, and Trinh’s response to future bypassing. A plausible but unproven hypothesis is that conditions may become more disruptive temporarily as Justin’s informal authority is constrained.

**What must not change.** Quality, helping clients succeed, and caring about the team.

**Justin and AI execution.** Alignment between Trinh and Justin is weak. Justin and Dalien often operate with little visible progress or transparency. Tam has directly told both that she has low confidence in the current HITL Eval effort. After reviewing Tam’s pivot proposal, Trinh asked her in writing to lead the new direction, working with Trinh, Shawn, and Lingan; a kickoff is scheduled. Trinh has said she will end the existing Justin-led effort but has not committed to timing. Tam has made explicit that URI cannot afford two shadow AI initiatives, and Trinh has acknowledged the issue. Until the existing effort receives a dated disposition and the decision is communicated, the new mandate remains vulnerable to competing work and authority. Panel Pro is also repeatedly named as a business risk, with the dynamics notes estimating roughly seven years of investment and about $400K per year in staff cost without demonstrated return.

**Talent and management.** Early interviews surface material performance-management and retention risks. Shawn is described as performing well in important areas but not addressing poor performance. Two research managers are characterized by colleagues as serious performance problems; those reports still need to be translated into observable behavior, prior feedback, business impact, and documented expectations before conclusions are reached. The recruiting manager is seen as effective leading her team but less effective helping the broader business. One strong researcher’s compensation has also been flagged as a possible retention risk. These are signals requiring evidence and accountable management action, not yet settled judgments about individuals.

**The AI / GTM strategic truth.** Competing broadly in HITL evaluation labor puts URI against Scale, Mercor, Surge, and similar labs that own frontier-lab spend. URI’s survey signals an evaluation gap, but about 3% outside-evaluator use does not prove willingness to pay for that SKU. More importantly, URI’s natural buyer is typically a UX or research leader. Serious model-eval and HITL buyers are usually CTO, Chief AI Officer, or VP Product. Chasing generic HITL means rebuilding GTM muscle URI does not currently have.

**The sharper bet.** Own independent human–AI readiness assurance for consequential mid-market B2B workflows (finance, insurance, healthcare), not a menu of HITL modules. AI should be core to growth strategy, not a separate lab or side initiative. Weakest evidence gap remains willingness to pay for that exact readiness offer.

## How Tam has helped shape the future

Tam has helped move the emerging direction from a diffuse “do AI stuff / sell HITL catalog” story toward a more coherent commercial and leadership thesis. This remains proposed direction rather than an executed transformation.

**Diagnosis.** She built a living dynamics picture (Ken, Trinh, Brooke, Shawn, Jeff, Dan/Riley) so leadership conversations rest on pattern, not anecdote. She named the accountability, vision, concentration, and Justin-opacity problems early.

**Strategy and offer.** She challenged weak brochure and research framing, filed interpretation caveats, and drove the pivot: stop competing as generic HITL labor; own human–AI workflow readiness; educate the market; prove with pilots and partners before a platform bet. She framed AI as the growth engine and innovation as concrete buckets (GTM, services, tooling inward-first; Five Lenses IP; panel intelligence; partners; Research Hub), not a vague AI lab.

**Leadership and change.** She prepared Dan and Trinh conversations with evidence, talking points that preserve Justin’s dignity while still creating room for a new mandate, and decision questions. She brought Collins/Porras into the vision workshop (preserve the core / stimulate progress) so the company can replace flowery vision with something people can actually rally to. She drafted GTM strategy for Trinh’s team and a possible Tam–Shawn path around the November sales handoff. Justin’s future lane remains unresolved and must be decided by Trinh rather than treated as settled in the advisory work.

**Role clarity.** She refused to be a side dish on an opaque Justin/Dalien track. She packaged her seat as **Executive Advisor, Strategy & Growth** (Advisor to the CEO): strategy, integrated growth including AI, client relations, and leadership mentoring, with clear decision rights and PE-recognizable value-creation outcomes. No “fractional” language.

## Through-line

URI needs a concrete, non-flowery direction and an operating system capable of carrying it. The leading diagnosis is an incomplete founder-to-CEO operating transition: Trinh has the formal authority, owner backing, team legitimacy, and demonstrated capability to lead, but has not yet consistently enforced that authority where Justin is concerned. The AI growth hypothesis that currently fits the firm is independent human–AI readiness, not competing with Scale/Mercor on eval labor, but willingness to pay remains unproven. Sales accountability, client diversification, talent management, and cross-functional visibility also need to improve. Tam’s value is as the CEO’s strategy and growth advisor who helps make commitments, decision rights, evidence, and accountability explicit—without becoming a substitute CEO or operator for work internal leaders must own.
