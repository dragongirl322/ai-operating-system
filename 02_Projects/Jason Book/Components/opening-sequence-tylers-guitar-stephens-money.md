# Opening Sequence: Tyler's Guitar / Stephen's Money

Working beat map for the novel's opening sequence.

## Central Question

Why does a good kid looking up to Jack make him feel like he is dying?

## Sequence Job

This sequence introduces Jack's gift before his damage, lets his body reveal what his mind cannot yet explain, and shows Stephen's control as plausible concern and financial superiority. The sequence should end with Simone recognizing that Stephen is part of the panic even though Jack has not named him that way.

## Core Tracks

**Body track:** Jack's body reacts before his conscious mind can interpret the danger.

**Power track:** Music briefly gives Jack authority and connection; Stephen's arrival restores the family hierarchy and turns Jack back into the unstable dependent.

**Theme track:** Jack's silence and self-blame protect Stephen's version of reality. The body knows there is more to the story.

## Scene 1: Tyler's Garage

### Beat 1: The Riff Under The Pine

Jack is standing outside under the big pine tree, finishing a cigarette, when he hears Tyler Holt playing the riff from Thin Lizzy's "The Boys Are Back in Town" in the garage next door. Jack recognizes both the song and the player. Tyler's garage door is open, and Jack walks over.

**Jack's internal shift:** Music draws him out of himself before he has time to decide whether he wants to be seen.

**Story function:** Shows that Jack still responds to music instinctively. Establishes Tyler as part of Jack's ordinary world and not just a plot device.

### Beat 2: Jack Offers A Specific Note

Jack steps into the open garage and tells Tyler it sounds pretty good. He offers one precise, generous suggestion about the strum pattern on part of the beat. The correction should reveal expertise without humiliating Tyler.

**Possible line energy:** "You're rushing the upstroke there. Let it drag half a second."

**Jack's internal shift:** He becomes useful for a moment. He remembers what it feels like to know something with his body.

**Story function:** Shows Jack's kindness, musical authority, and ability to mentor without dominating.

### Beat 3: Tyler Hands Him The Guitar

Tyler says his mom told him Jack played in a band. Jack deflects, but Tyler asks him to play while already handing him the guitar. "No" is not really an option, not because Tyler is pushy in a cruel way, but because his admiration is open and disarming.

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack wants the guitar and fears wanting it. He has to decide whether to touch the part of himself he misses.

**Story function:** Positions music as home and threat. Tyler's admiration starts to expose the wound.

### Beat 4: Mercy Static Surfaces

Jack takes the guitar and plays a few bars of Metallica's "Master of Puppets." The song choice works as a recognizable 1986 tether and an unconscious thematic flare: control, manipulation, and the body moving under another force. Jack plays beautifully enough that Tyler immediately understands he is not just some guy who "used to be in a band." He was real.

**Jack's internal shift:** For a few seconds, he is Mercy Static's Jack Tate again: musician, craftsman, someone with command.

**Story function:** Shows the reader Jack's lost self directly, instead of explaining it.

### Beat 5: The Look

Tyler looks at Jack with open admiration and says something like, "Dude, you're legit." His face is alive, vulnerable, and full of possibility. Jack sees the boy before damage, the boy who still believes talent can become a future.

**Jack's internal shift:** Tyler's admiration stops feeling good and starts feeling unbearable. Jack feels exposed by the boy's hope.

**Story function:** Turns admiration into a trauma trigger. The reader starts to understand that Jack's body recognizes danger before his mind can name it.

### Beat 6: The Hand Shake

Jack's hand starts to shake. The chord fails or buzzes. He tries to cover it with a joke and hands the guitar back before Tyler can fully understand what changed.

**Jack's internal shift:** Shame floods the moment. He has lost control of the one thing that briefly made him whole.

**Story function:** Makes the body the first witness. The panic begins in a small visible failure, not an explained flashback.

### Beat 7: Stephen's Arrival

Jack sees Stephen Tate's car pull up across the street. Stephen's arrival gives Jack a socially acceptable reason to leave the garage. Jack tells Tyler he has to go and crosses back toward his house.

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack thinks he is escaping the panic by leaving Tyler's gaze, but he is walking toward the deeper trigger.

**Story function:** Stephen interrupts Jack's almost-reconnection with music and restores the old family pressure.

## Scene 2: Jack's House

### Beat 8: Jack Performs Normal

Jack greets Stephen and brings him inside the house next door to Tyler and Megan. Jack offers him a drink, possibly a beer, trying to make the visit ordinary.

**Optional Stephen move:** Stephen declines with a mild superiority edge: "Little early for me."

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack starts managing himself, trying to look steady in front of Stephen.

**Story function:** Shows Jack's learned performance around his brother. The threat does not need to be loud to control him.

### Beat 9: The Empty Fridge

When Jack opens the fridge, Stephen sees there is no real food. The moment gives Stephen a point of entry. He does not immediately attack; he observes, files it, and lets Jack feel the observation.

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack knows Stephen has seen something that can be used as evidence against him.

**Story function:** Turns ordinary poverty and disorder into family-court-style evidence of Jack's inadequacy.

### Beat 10: Concern As Control

After some small talk, Stephen says he has to go. He sets cash on the counter and tells Jack to buy food. Real food, not garbage, strings, or cigarettes.

**Possible line energy:** "Go buy food. Actual food. Not strings, not smokes, not whatever gets you through an afternoon."

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack feels both need and humiliation. Refusing the money would be pride; taking it confirms Stephen's story.

**Story function:** Shows Stephen's cruelty in the form of practical help. The money is not generosity; it is narrative control.

### Beat 11: The Simone Line

Stephen comments on Simone before leaving.

**Possible line:** "Simone's a good woman. Don't make her carry you too."

**Jack's internal shift:** Stephen names Jack's private fear: that he is a burden to Simone, one more damaged thing she has to manage.

**Story function:** Stephen lands the cleanest wound while still sounding reasonable. The word "too" implies everyone has always had to carry Jack.

## Scene 3: Aftermath With Simone

### Beat 12: The Body Breaks

As soon as Stephen is gone, Jack's hands start shaking again. His eyes water. His heart pounds. He cannot breathe. He knows it is a panic attack, and that recognition makes it no easier to stop.

**Jack's internal shift:** His performed normal collapses once the threat leaves.

**Story function:** Shows that Jack can survive the direct interaction, but his body pays afterward.

### Beat 13: Simone Finds Him

Simone comes home and finds Jack on the bedroom floor, rocking through the panic attack. She recognizes what is happening and goes to him without treating him like a spectacle.

**Jack's internal shift:** Simone's presence gives him enough safety to speak, but not enough clarity to understand what caused the attack.

**Story function:** Establishes Simone as witness and possible mentor: steady, practical, emotionally intelligent.

### Beat 14: Jack Names The Wrong Trigger

Jack tells Simone about playing Tyler's guitar. He says Tyler looked at him with admiration, like Jack was somebody, like he had something Tyler wanted. Jack says the kid is good and should not look up to him because Jack is weak and has nothing. He likes Tyler and feels protective of him, but Tyler's aliveness and vulnerability did something to him.

**Possible line energy:** "It was the way he looked at me. Like I was somebody. Like I had something he wanted."

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack can describe the emotional trigger but not the deeper reason it exists.

**Story function:** Keeps the central question alive: why does being admired by a vulnerable boy make Jack panic?

### Beat 15: Stephen Becomes An Afterthought

Jack casually mentions, almost as an afterthought, "Oh yeah, and Stephen stopped by." He presents Stephen's visit as incidental, as if it had nothing to do with the panic attack.

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack unconsciously protects Stephen's place in the story. He can blame his own weakness more easily than he can name Stephen as a threat.

**Story function:** Shows how deeply Jack has been trained to misread the family system.

### Beat 16: Simone Sees The Money

Simone sees the cash on the counter. She does not need to explain everything aloud. The money tells her Stephen happened inside the panic, not beside it.

**Jack's internal shift:** Jack may not even register her looking at the money, or he may try to explain it away as help.

**Story function:** Final button. Simone sees the pattern before Jack can name it. The scene ends with a quiet piece of evidence: Stephen's "help" has left Jack on the floor.

## Final Image

Simone standing between Jack's panic and Stephen's cash on the counter, understanding that the story Jack told her is incomplete.

## Drafting Notes

- Keep Tyler's dialogue natural and teen-simple. He should sound like a real kid, not a symbolic figure.
- Keep Stephen polished. His lines should be defensible if quoted back, but devastating in context.
- Do not explain the trauma yet. Let the reader experience the sequence as a pattern of body response, shame, power, and misattribution.
- Let music carry tenderness before it carries menace.
- The scene should make the reader wonder why Jack's body reacts so violently to admiration, vulnerability, and Stephen's concern.
