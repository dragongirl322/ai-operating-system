## The Book

**Working Title:** Before It Was Urgent

**Subtitle (working):** Nine Skills Design and Research Leaders Should Have Been Building All Along — and Can't Afford to Ignore Now

**Core Thesis:** The nine skills this book covers are not new. The fields that need them have spent decades developing them in the wrong context, celebrating fluency in the language without ever measuring whether they produced outcomes, and building capability divorced from the business situations where it actually matters. AI has not created this gap. It has made the cost of the gap undeniable.

**The argument in one sentence:** These skills have always mattered; AI just eliminated the hiding places.

---

## What the Book Is and Isn't

**It is:**

- A practical guide for senior ICs, team leads, and directors in design and user research
- Focused on the skills to build, the outcomes those skills drive, and why they matter now
- High-level guidance on where to start — enough to act, not a step-by-step manual
- Anchored in design and research with enough precision and clarity that practitioners in adjacent disciplines recognize themselves in it

**It is not:**

- A detailed how-to manual
- A book about AI tools or how to use them
- Another leadership framework book written by a coach or consultant
- A broad technical leadership book trying to speak to everyone in the room

---

## Audience

**Primary:** Senior individual contributors through Director/Senior Director level in design and user research (including UX research, product design, content design, and adjacent craft roles focused on the human experience of technology) — people who have been rewarded for technical and craft excellence and are now in roles where that is no longer sufficient, and who haven't been told clearly what is.

**Secondary:** People leaders at the VP level who need a shared language and framework for developing their design and research teams.

**What unites them:** They are technically and creatively credible. They are organizationally frustrated. They can feel the gap between the work they produce and the impact it has, but they can't always name why.

**What this book does for them:** Names the gap. Makes the argument. Gives them a place to start.

**On crossover reach:** The book is written for design and research practitioners. It does not address engineering, PM, or other technical disciplines directly. The specificity is intentional — it is what makes the argument land. Practitioners in adjacent disciplines who read it will recognize themselves in it anyway. That is the model: anchor deeply, travel broadly.

---

## Market Positioning

The existing market is crowded with books about how leaders should _use_ AI — mindset shifts, AI adoption frameworks, integration playbooks. The most credible recent entry (HBR Press, _More Human_) argues that leaders who thrive will use AI to become more human.

None of that is this book.

This book does not argue that AI changes leadership. It argues that AI exposes the leadership development failures that have always existed in technical fields — skills developed in low-stakes, discipline-specific containers, celebrated without measurement, and never transferred to the business contexts where they actually determine outcomes. The credential that makes this argument credible is operational: the author has built these capabilities inside complex enterprises and measured whether they worked. That distinguishes this from books written by coaches, strategists, and researchers.

**The white space:** A practitioner-to-director level guide, written by an operator, anchored in design and research, that treats the skills gap as a systems and context failure rather than an individual development failure. Specific enough to resonate deeply with its primary audience. Clear enough to travel beyond it.

## The Nine Skills

The following are drawn from the article _AI Is in the Room. Are You?_ and form the chapter architecture of the book.

**Foundation (not a chapter — the condition beneath all nine):** Mastery — not as a destination but as a constant orientation toward the work. Without it, the nine skills are transactional. With it, they compound.

9. Storytelling and Executive Communication

## Chapter Architecture

Each chapter is built from these elements. The order, weight, and structure flex per chapter — the argument determines the form, not the other way around.

**Required elements (not a fixed sequence):**

- **A failure story** — A pattern of failure drawn from design and research practice. This is not illustrative. It is the reckoning that makes the reader uncomfortable before the argument lands. Some chapters open here. Others may establish the skill first so the failure story hits harder.
    
- **The skill defined** — Named clearly, without jargon. Described in terms of what it produces, not what it involves.
    
- **The wrong-context argument** — Why technical fields have been developing this skill in a container that was never designed to produce business outcomes. The celebration without measurement. The capability without transfer. This is the book's sharpest and most original claim — make it here, per skill.
    
- **The outcome-oriented version** — What it looks like when this skill is developed in the right context, measured against the right outcomes, and practiced at the level the moment requires. Concrete. Grounded in design and research practice.
    
- **Where to start** — Not a curriculum. A first move. Specific enough that a reader knows what to do differently before the next chapter.

## Voice and Writing Standards

This book is written by Tammy Snow. Every sentence should sound like her.

### Voice Attributes

|Attribute|What It Means in Practice|
|---|---|
|**Precise**|No over-explaining. Say it once, say it well.|
|**Grounded**|Evidence and logic first. Claims need to stand up.|
|**Direct**|Point first, then rationale. Never bury the lead.|
|**Warm without softness**|Care is present but it doesn't dilute the honesty.|
|**Edgy**|Has a point of view and is willing to defend it. Not provocative for its own sake.|
|**Human**|Sounds like a real person who thinks carefully, not a corporate voice that hedges everything.|

### Language to Use

- Specific over vague: "reduced review cycles by 40%" beats "made real progress"
- Active voice: "I decided" not "a decision was made"
- Direct questions: "What's your take?" not "I was wondering if you might have thoughts"
- Honest framing: "Here's what I know, here's what I'm still working through"

### Language to Cut

- Corporate filler: _synergy, leverage, circle back, at the end of the day, move the needle_
- Over-qualification: _I could be wrong, but... / This might not be the right idea..._
- Declared passion: _I'm incredibly passionate about..._ — passion is shown, not announced
- Vague superlatives: _world-class, innovative, best-in-class_ without evidence
- Excessive hedging of any kind
- Em dashes: one per piece, maximum. Use only when nothing else will do.

### The Five Questions Before Any Section Ships

1. What is the one thing I want them to walk away knowing?
2. Am I leading with the point, or burying it?
3. Is there a word I can cut without losing meaning?
4. Am I hedging? Why?
5. Does this sound like me, or like what I think I should sound like?

## Decisions Made (From Project Conversation)

These are the decisions landed on in the process of developing this project. Do not re-litigate them without a clear reason.

**On the thesis:** The nine skills are not new. Technical fields have spent decades developing them in the wrong context — building capability in discipline-specific containers, celebrating fluency without measuring outcomes, and never transferring the skills to the business situations where they determine results. AI has not created the gap. It has made the cost of the gap impossible to ignore.

**On the AI framing:** AI is the deadline, not the cause. The framing that gives this book durability: these skills have always mattered; AI just eliminated the hiding places. Avoid framing that makes the book read as a moment-in-time AI response.

**On audience:** Senior IC through Director/Senior Director in design and user research. Anchored in design and research practice — not written for all technical disciplines. Specificity is the strategy: a precisely targeted argument travels further than a broad one. Not written for experienced VPs/SVPs. Targets people who are technically and creatively excellent, organizationally frustrated, and can feel the gap but can't name it.

**On chapter structure:** Elements, not a template. Each chapter contains a failure story, skill definition, wrong-context argument, outcome-oriented version, and a first move. The order and weight flex per chapter based on what serves the argument. The reader should not be able to predict the structure by chapter three.

**On title:** _Before It Was Urgent_ — authoritative and clean, edging toward bold. The title implies the author saw this coming before the field did. That is a credibility statement built into the name.

**On market position:** This is not a book about using AI. It is not a mindset book. It is an operator's argument, written by someone who built these capabilities inside complex enterprises and measured whether they worked. The credential is the differentiator.

**On discipline scope:** The book is anchored in design and research. Failure stories, examples, and applications are drawn from design and research practice — this is where the argument is sharpest and the author's credibility is strongest. When examples from Tammy's career are used, they are framed as operator stories that illuminate the skill, not design showcases. The specificity of the anchor is what allows the argument to travel to readers in adjacent disciplines.
