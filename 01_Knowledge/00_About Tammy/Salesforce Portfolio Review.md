# FRAME · 3.5 minutes

> In his book The Blueprint, Doug Conant says your life story is your leadership story. I
> 
> I learned to be a leader early in life, as the oldest of six kids in a hard-working household. Five people watched everything I did. I couldn't get anything done alone. None of them had to listen to me. Example, delegation, influence without authority. I learned all three there.
> 
> I'm a lifelong athlete. All-American in high school. All-Region in three sports in college. Sport taught me three leadership lessons. Strive for excellence and keep improving. And lead by showing you're willing to work as hard as everyone else. Be there to elevate the team, not myself.
> 
> In my career, I've spent twenty-five years a people leader. Individuals. Teams. Leaders of leaders. Organizations at scale.
> 
> Leading leaders was the hard crossing. What changed the most was proximity. I could no longer carry all of the details about depth work, and holding it anyway made my leaders smaller. The work became building a vision an entire organization could carry, then making my leaders great at carrying it.
> 
> What I've learned over and over again in my life is that what produces results is elevating other people, and ambition for the cause instead of for myself.
> 
> I lead through trust, build for capability rather than dependence, and hold a hard line on clarity that's grounded in evidence — because that's what makes an organization better after I leave than it was when I arrived.
> 
> Most recently, Workday. I led a global, multidiscipline UX team of 300 across Workday's entire suite of products. For a bit of additional context that will help the stories I share make more sense, Workday has 3 primary businesses and a platform organization (show diagram). From the beginning of my leadership, my org worked in a centralized partnership where the majority of our designers and researchers were embedded with product pillars and a smaller percentage worked horizontally, e.g., design system and measurement. 
> 
> Two stories that show you how I show up as a leader.

# STORY ONE · GETTING READY FOR AI

## 1 · The claim

>When Workday's AI push started, I didn't ask for budget or headcount. I started small and organic, and I only spent real capacity once the need had proven itself.

That turned out to be the right way to build it, and it's the through-line of this story. Start small. Prove it. Then resource it.

## 2 · Diagnose

>  In conversations with product leadership during this timeframe, the group GMs and our CPO, I recognized two problems my team was well suited to help with.
> 
> First, there was real concern about how to decide where AI would be valuable, and how to make sure we applied the right kind of AI to a given problem. Generative, Conversational, anticipatory, agentic. Those are different tools and they solve different things.
> 
> Second, our product teams had always worked at the feature level. That's how they scoped, staffed, and shipped. And there was general recognition among those leaders that agentic AI doesn't work that way. An agent has to understand the whole journey and what the user is actually trying to accomplish. A team that thinks in features can't build that.
> 
> Nobody asked my organization to solve either one. I heard both problems in the course of those conversations and recognized we already had what they needed.
> 
>  We knew who the users were, what they were trying to achieve, and how they went about achieving it. My team built the user typology and jobs-to-be-done framework when I first arrived at Workday, years before any of this, because the company talked in features instead of user outcomes. We'd already had success helping teams operate beyond feature silos and instead focusing on complete flows.
> 
> We'd already proven we could help teams design cohesive solutions across multiple features, because we'd applied that framework. The clearest example is work we had led the previous year to address key manager jobs to be done and to improve Findability. That included a fix to one of the most difficult manager tasks that cut the time to move an employee from one location to another by 47%.
> 
> So we were ready when the moment called for it. The capability gap that was about to slow the company down was the one my team already had.

## 3 · Architect

> While we had the capabilities the company needed, we hadn't yet applied it to delivering AI. This happened in stages, over about two years. 

**Early 2023. Organic, almost free.**

I pulled two thought leaders, one designer and one researcher, off the edges of their day jobs to run organic learning and research on AI. And I tasked the designer with defining human-centered AI guidelines. We didn't structure this as a team wide initiative or reorg rather as an organic effort with one clear deliverable, the AI UX guidelines.

Those guidelines were the principles. What we believe about how AI should treat a human. They landed in the summer of 2023, before Workday's big customer event, Rising.

**April 2024. The principles weren't enough.**

By then it was clear our product teams were struggling with something the guidelines didn't solve. They didn't know how to _think_ about AI. Principles tell you what to value. They don't tell a team where to start.

So I wrote a thought piece and handed it to that organic group with an assignment. Turn this into a guidance kit product teams can actually use.

The kit did what the guidelines couldn't. For each AI modality it defined the interaction paradigms and patterns — when, where, and how AI shows up for a user. How often. The content and visual patterns. And the feedback mechanisms that let a user's response refine the underlying model. Wherever it made sense, it lived as documented guidance and design system tokens, not as a memo.

V1 went to teams in September 2024.

I want to be precise about my role, because it's the pattern I'd point to. I wrote the piece that set the direction. My team built the system that made it usable. I didn't build the kit. I made sure the kit got built, and that it got built from the right point of view.

**Early 2025. Now I spend the capacity.**

Once V1 had proven the need and the appetite, I carved off a small horizontal AI team. Ten people. No new headcount.

I picked them on two things. They were already early adopters of AI tools on their own, and they'd expressed real interest in working in that space. Appetite and aptitude were the available signal and they turned out to be the right ones. Their job was to keep the guidance current and drive adoption across the product teams.

Before moving these resources to the horizontal team, I went to the group GMs and we aligned on which projects we could deprioritize in order to allocate resources to AI--we all agreed that a horizontal team focused on guidelines, paradigms, patterns made sense. And we agreed up front on what success looked like, and it wasn't design output. It was whether their teams shipped AI that was valuable, trustworthy, and cohesive. I wanted to be measured on their outcome, not mine.

Jim Collins calls it firing bullets before cannonballs. Two people and a designer in 2023. A thought piece and a kit in 2024. Small, cheap, low-risk — until the pattern proved itself. Then the cannonball: a funded team in 2025.

The way the central team related to the rest of the organization is an important part of this story. 

The central team owned the guidelines, the guidance, the patterns, and the design system components. 

That last one needed teeth. Guidance tells a team how to build well. It doesn't stop something bad from shipping. So we defined an AI experience checklist — a specific, agreed set of things an AI experience had to clear before it went to a customerand we got it written into definition of done, which meant clearing was a gate.

## 4 · Align

### Agent Factory

> > Product leadership made a move at the same time. They knew they didn't have the engineering and product talent to deliver AI at the speed we needed, so they stood up a team called Agent Factory. They put the founder and CEO of one of our AI acquisitions in charge and gave him headcount to hire engineers and product managers who had actually built AI before.
> 
> Two centralized teams, both with a claim on every product team in the company. That can result in a turf war. It went the other way.
> 
> First meeting, he told me he'd heard from the head of financials and a few other leaders that mine was the most successful matrixed organization at Workday. Then he asked me how it was structured. 
> 
> So I walked him through it. Central team owns the standard. Embedded leaders own adoption. Contribution model pushes what the teams learn back up.
> 
> He told me he was going to build Agent Factory the same way. He did. That model allowed Agent Factory team to scale more effectively while simultaneously building better relationships with product teams and leveraging subject matter and domain expertise.
> 
> We also aligned on decision rights to ensure we avoided unnecessary conflict down the road. 
> 
> My organization held authority on experience quality. His held authority on data quality and how the agents were built and structured from an ML standpoint.
> 
> We named that early, before either of us had a reason to defend any territory.

### Bottom-up adoption

> With an org structure, an operating model, and alignment in place, executing the work became the job.
> 
> My embedded directors knew their role. Know the central guidance and patterns, apply them along with your domain expertise, contribute back what the central team hadn't covered. And they were empowered and expected to hold the quality bar on AI experiences.
> 
> That last part wasn't mine to grant alone. I'd aligned with Agent Factory and with the group GMs that design held authority on experience quality. So when one of my directors pushed back in a room, she wasn't offering an opinion. She was doing the job this company's leadership had agreed she would do.
> 
> That worked most of the time. Not all of the time.
> 
> We were building an agent in financials. There was an execution review, and the prototype that got shown wasn't ours. A product manager had built it. It used none of the guidance, none of the patterns, none of the components in the design system.
> 
> The design leader in the room raised it. Short conversation, no resolution. It came up to my senior director, and he brought it to me because he read it as bigger than one prototype. The alignment Agent Factory and I had made at the top hadn't traveled down.
> 
> I sent a note to the head of Agent Factory and his VP of Product. Laid out what happened and asked how we could align so it didn't happen again. They didn't want it happening either. I recommended we bring in the VP leading the financials agent work, since he was the one accountable, and he agreed with where we landed.
> 
> That team never did it again. Other teams did.
> 
> The honest diagnosis is that this was cultural. Product teams at Workday were used to being the final decision maker. We were asking them to build against a standard they hadn't set, and some of them experienced that as losing something.
> 
> I want to say why that incident mattered to me more than the outcome did.
> 
> When I got to Workday, this organization had learned not to escalate. Years of it. They'd watched quality get negotiated away and concluded they weren't the ones who got to hold the line. I spent a lot of effort with my leadership team building a culture where escalating is the right move and gets treated that way. Not tolerated. Expected, when it's warranted.
> 
> So a designer said something in the room. Her director carried it. My senior director recognized it as systemic and brought it to me.
> 
> That's the culture working. That's the second story, and I'll come back to it.

### The chat panel

> One more from the bottom-up side, and this one went the other direction.
> 
> Two teams, one from financials and one from HCM, told us they were going to build their own conversational chat panel. Their reason was legitimate. The platform-level panel, built to design system rules, was too small for what they needed.
> 
> That's the moment where a standard either bends or breaks. Two teams going their own way on the primary AI surface would have ended it.
> 
> I told my head of design to go align with the relevant GMs, get their actual requirements, and take those to the platform team to see what we could do. Not to defend the pattern. To find out if the pattern was wrong.
> 
> It was. We redefined the pattern and the platform component so the panel was resizable, dynamically and by the user. Quickly.
> 
> I'd point at that one over the financials escalation if you asked me where we had real influence. Two GMs brought us a problem and got a platform change instead of a policy argument.

### Top-down adoption

> Top down is what actually moved the number.
> 
> I picked the person in my organization who was best at telling the story of the work. Not the most senior. The one who could explain what to do, why, and how, and have a room believe him.
> 
> The two of us got ourselves invited to the business-level leadership meetings. Core HCM and financials. That wasn't a hard sell. The group GMs were glad to have us, because they understood we were bringing their organizations a gift. Three reasons I had the designer present instead  instead of me: He was closer to the work than I was and could speak to it with more precision and more conviction than I could. He was looking for growth and stretch, and this was one I could hand him. And he'd earned it.
>
## 5 · Measurement

> I want to back up, because none of this works without something we built years earlier.
> 
> When I joined Workday, one of the things I was asked to do was figure out how to measure experience. What I found was that the company collected several billion events a day and still could not tell you what a user actually did in the product. Enormous data. No observation.
> 
> So I hired a small data science team. Data engineers, data scientists, analysts, a product manager, under a director who reported to me. I gave them one job. Make it possible to see what users are doing.
> 
> They built the infrastructure, the pipelines, and the visualization layer. That was years before AI and it had nothing to do with AI.
> 
> When I stood up the AI team, I went back to that group. I worked with my director of analytics on a strategy for measuring the value of an agent, which is a harder question than measuring whether someone used a feature. I coached. He built the strategy and led his team through executing it.
> 
> By the time we shipped our first organically built agents, that system was already running.
> 
> Two things came out of it I did not plan.
> 
> The first is that it became how our CEO reports agent performance to analysts, to the board, and to the market.
> 
> I didn't know that had happened. My director of analytics sent me a note telling me our CEO had just used our data on an earnings call.
> 
> That's the proudest I've been at Workday. When I joined, the company wasn't talking about product data at all. It couldn't. And the reason it could now was my vision, his strategy, and his team's execution. In that order, and none of the three works without the other two. 
> 
> The second is the one I'd want you to remember. When Workday decided to move to consumption-based pricing, there was a moment of _we have to go build the data system for this._ Then somebody realized it already existed. My organization had built it, for a completely different reason, years before anyone needed it for that.
> 
> A business model change didn't get delayed because my organization had been measuring the right thing since 2022.
> 


## 7 · Deliver

> Where it landed.
> 
> Twenty-plus agents by the time I left. Not all generally available, but every one in front of at least early adopters.
> 
> Every one built on the same guidelines, patterns, and design system components. 
> Every one measured in the same framework.
> 
> Every one gated by the experience checklist, written into definition of done.
> 
> I reviewed almost none of them personally.
> 
> Delivery timelines came down roughly fifty percent. Usage of had increased by ~73% across agents. Importantly, multi-turn conversations and return visits had also increased by over 50% across agents.
> 
> Our first organic agent, self-service across employee and manager tasks, shipped in three months. Historically that took over a year.
> 
> The reason it sent so fast is because of strong alignment between my organization, Agent Factory, and the platform team, decision rights that were already settled, and a group of people that were committed to make it happen.
> 
> My part was three things. Making sure my team understood that moving fast was the expectation, not a compromise. Making sure we had evidence fast enough to be useful, which meant research that returned in days. And holding the quality bar while both of those were true.
> 
> They defined how and they executed. I set the direction, supported it, and coached where it helped.
> 
> At our most recent analyst event, Workday showed agents across product areas, user types, and acquired products. The assessment was that both the solutions and the narrative were cohesive. That doesn't happen by accident across that many teams.

## 8 · What I got wrong

> Last thing on this story, and it's the part I got wrong.
> 
> Go back to the beginning, when the CPO asked me for guidance on building AI that delivered real value. That relationship was real. He trusted my read.
> 
> The answer I came back with was to do fewer things extremely well. He was running an effort focused almost entirely on quantity.
> 
> I had evidence. Research showed most customers weren't ready to adopt at the pace we were building. The agents being prioritized weren't likely to deliver value. And I was hearing privately from product leaders that their teams didn't have the skills to build what was being asked of them.
> 
> I raised it diplomatically. One concern at a time, in writing, over weeks. I waited for him to invite the bold assertion, and that invitation never came.
> 
> The gift of hindsight tells me it would have been worth traveling to his office, in Boulder, to have an in person meeting to share the complete picture: the problem, the risk, a proposal.
> 
> The answer isn't that I needed better evidence. I had the evidence.
> 
> I was holding back. I was so focused on being diplomatic that I didn't lead boldly in a moment that required it. I chose the version of myself that was easier to be in the room with.
> 
> What I know now is that at the top, the architecture of how you deliver a hard truth carries as much weight as the truth does. Fragmented communication signals peripheral concern. A complete, in-person case signals organizational urgency.
> 
> Where I used this lesson was in advocating for a release of our first organic agent to be held because it didn't hit our quality bar. I showed up in person. I went in with the complete picture: the rubric, the user research, my team's evidence, and Agent Factory aligned with me. 
>

# STORY TWO · REBUILDING THE ORGANIZATION

## 1 · The claim

> I was hired to build a research practice and a measurement system at Workday. Neither one existed.
> 
> About a year in, the design VP left and I was asked to take on design as well.
> 
> Early on, one of our group GMs told me that earning back trust was going to be impossible for me. Three design leaders before me had made commitments and not kept them. He was clear it wasn't about me. It was about what design leadership had done to that relationship over several years.
> 
> The situation wasn't healthy. I believed I could fix it.

## 2 · Diagnose

> Here's what I inherited.
> 
> A product culture that used design as hands. People to execute someone else's idea. Design wasn't empowered anywhere in it.
> 
> A company that made decisions on intuition and anecdote instead of reliable insight and data. That's part of why I was hired in the first place.
> 
> A design team that had gone years without a clear vision or objectives connected to what the company and the product organization were actually trying to do.
> 
> No trust between design leadership and product and engineering leadership. No credibility. Reorgs and priority shifts had landed in a vacuum for years, so there was no reason to believe the next commitment would hold.
> 
> And no clear expectations set within the design team. Nobody had built an environment where excellence, development, and quality were the standard, and people were being measured against a bar nobody had named.
> 
> Underneath all of it, a legacy technology stack that created real design constraints.
> 
> So why did I think I could fix it, when three people before me hadn't.
> 
> I'd been watching the dynamic for a year and listening to our designers. I sat in their design reviews. I gave them direct, honest feedback and I could see they appreciated it. Enough of them told me they wanted to work for me that I stopped treating it as a compliment and started treating it as data.
> 
> When the change happened, they were thrilled. Many of them actively advocated for me to be their permanent leader. So did a number of our product leaders.
> 
> The quantifiable version came after. We ran employee sentiment weekly, and within two months of my taking the team it was up more than fifty percent. Some of that was relief. A change after a bad stretch produces a bump no matter who shows up.
> 
> But it held. Through layoffs. Through centralizing the teams. A dip, a fast rebound, then steady. 

## 3 · Architect

> Five things, in this order. Each one was the precondition for the next.
> 
> **Transparency first.** I came to Workday sending a weekly email about what I was working on. Started that as the research leader and never stopped. I held AMAs and asked for the hard questions.
> 
> What I told them was this. There are some things I'm not allowed to share, for a variety of reasons. If you ask me a question I can't answer, I'll be candid about that.
> 
> **Then a clear strategy.** An org-wide strategy with objectives, delivered in my first 90 days, tied directly to what the company and the product organization were trying to do. 
> 
> Every person could find themselves in it. I made the what and the why unmistakable. I did not tell them how. An organization that's been treated as hands doesn't become an organization of designers because you say so. It becomes one when you hand it a problem and get out of the way.
> 
> **Then cross-functional trust.** I had this already as the research leader, so this wasn't building from nothing. It was making sure every one of those leaders knew they'd get the same from me as a design leader, and more.
> 
> That one was a mechanism, not a posture. I was in the room when the group GMs set their OKRs and I gave input. They reviewed mine and gave input back. I aligned with them on an engagement model so we could talk about resourcing and priorities as a matter of course rather than a series of emergencies. When something needed to change , it changed by mutual agreement. Never unilaterally, in either direction.
> 
> That last part is the whole thing. Every reorg and priority shift before me had been made in a vacuum. The fix wasn't a better decision. It was a standing agreement that no decision gets made that way again.
> 
> **Then expectations.** With our HR business partners I built new career frameworks, ten critical skills per discipline, promotion guidelines, and a calibration process.
> 
> I delegated the specifics to my discipline leaders. They defined what good looked like in their own practice and they owned it, because a standard handed down from me would have been one more thing happening to them.
> 
> **Then safety**, which could only happen once the other four were in place. People don't feel safe because you tell them they are. They feel safe when they know what's going on, know where they're headed, know what's expected, and watch you keep your word to people outside the room.
> 
> Then it's the human stuff. Regular 1:1s with my team. Quarterly town halls. Office hours twice a week, fifteen minutes, any level, any topic. Knowing every name and every face. Being the boss who hugs or fist-bumps.
> 
> One more thing that made all of it work. I showed up the same way every single time. Consistent, on brand, no version of me that changed depending on the room. My team and my partners knew what they were getting, always, and they knew my objective was for all of us to be successful. I never deviated from that.

## 4 · The decision to centralize

> Everything I just described was building toward one structure, whether or not it was going to happen.
> 
> Some UX teams reported to me. Others sat inside product organizations, and those had mostly come to us through acquisitions. Which meant designers got pointed at whatever the leader they reported to cared about most, and there was no way to hold a consistent standard across any of it.
> 
> Centralization had been proposed before and the President of Product and Technology had said no. The reason was trust, and at the time it wasn't mine to fix.
> 
> So I didn't ask for it. I built for it.
> 
> Then our CDO left, and within about three months the group GMs and the President of Product and Technology decided to centralize.
> 
> Same proposal. Same company. Different answer. I never made the case. That's what the trust was for.

## 5 · The hard call

> The decision was made. What was in question was timing, and that was mine.
> 
> It collided with one of the largest layoffs in Workday's history. So the order was the call. Centralize and then lay people off, or lay off and then centralize.
> 
> Neither is good. Centralize first and you move people to new teams and new managers and cut them weeks later, which tells everyone the structure was theater. Lay off first and I inherit teams that are already grieving and already braced for the next thing.
> 
> My SVP, our HR business partner, the group GMs and I worked it for a couple of weeks. We chose layoffs first. Part of the reasoning was to keep me from walking into a brand new organization and immediately cutting my own people.
> 
> The condition I needed was decision rights on which UX people stayed. If I was inheriting the aftermath, I needed to be the one who decided who was in it.

### The part that was actually hard

> I couldn't tell anyone on my leadership team about either one.
> 
> I wasn't going to lay people off without mapping the org structure first, because you can't make good cut decisions without knowing what you're building. But mapping a structure means talking to the leaders who'll be affected by it, and I couldn't.
> 
> So I did two things. I worked closely with the group GMs and their GMs, so I wasn't making these decisions alone or in a vacuum. And I relied on knowing enough about every team in my organization, and their real strengths and weaknesses, to make calls I could defend later.
> 
> The moment layoffs were done, I could talk about centralization. And there was one advantage to that order I hadn't fully anticipated. It meant my leadership team and the directors coming over from the decentralized teams were all in the room to finalize the structure. The people who had to live in it helped build it.

### The leaders coming to me

> Before the change, I met one on one with every leader moving to me. Not a group announcement. One at a time.
> 
> In each of those I told them what my role in the layoffs had been, including the reasoning behind decisions I'd made. Then I took their questions.
> 
> That's a hard forty-five minutes and I did it repeatedly. I was walking into an organization whose whole injury was that things happened to them without explanation. I wasn't going to open by doing that again.
> 
> One conversation mattered more than the others. I had a director I wasn't sure about. His prior scope had been narrow enough that I hadn't seen him do what I need a director to do.
> 
> I gave him both options. Stay at the scope he had, and here's the risk. Take an expanded scope, and here's the risk and here's what it opens up.
> 
> He took the expanded scope with clear eyes. He knew exactly what he was signing up for. He did a great job. Some of that was him. Some of it was that he finally had clear expectations, and some of it was that I coached his leader on how to support him.
> 
> That's the whole thesis in one person. He wasn't a weak director. He'd never been given real scope, a clear standard, or a manager who knew how to back him.

### The first six weeks

> An AMA immediately, for the hard questions. I wasn't waiting for people to settle down, because settled down mostly means quiet.
> 
> Weekly one-on-ones with every affected leader for four to six weeks. And intensive coaching with my head of design and my head of research, because the integration was going to work or fail on whether those two operated as one thing.

### Resistance

> Not everyone came along at first.
> 
> One of my design directors resisted hard. He'd been comfortable in a structure that didn't hold him to much, and he wanted authority over design for all of financials.
> 
> It was short-lived. Early on we promoted three people he had never been able to get promoted under the old structure. He watched that and drew his own conclusion. I didn't have to convince him. The system did.
> 
> The other came from a product VP upset he'd lost control of his design resources. We dug in and found he'd laid off his design resources. There was nothing to lose control of. My head of design handled that conversation and I coached him on how to talk about it, because the easy version embarrasses the VP and the useful version doesn't. He de-escalated it and the relationship held.

## 6 · Deliver

> Where it landed.
> 
> Fungibility, and it arrived exactly when we needed it. In a decentralized structure, designers get pointed at whatever the leader they report to cares about most. Centralized, I could put people against the highest priority in the business rather than the highest priority in a reporting line.
> 
> That mattered most in the AI pivot. The ten people I moved onto the AI team came out of somewhere, and I could only make that trade because the resources were mine to trade. The group GMs saw it and said so directly.
> 
> Quality went up. Consistent standard, real accountability for using the design system where it applied. And because we held teams accountable for contributing back, the system expanded to cover more of what they actually needed.
> 
> That system now spans 143 repositories and more than 700 reusable assets. A billion icon impressions a month. Over a million button interactions an hour. It's a public repository, so our ISVs, partners, and customers building on Workday are working from the same components our own teams are.
> 
> Performance went up, because every person in UX is measured against a consistent and specific standard. People get promoted for impact and for building skill. Not for doing someone's bidding.
> 
> On people, sentiment dipped through the change and then climbed and held. Attrition is a weak metric right now because the market is what it is and nobody's moving. What I'll say is that regrettable attrition was very low. We kept the people we meant to keep.
> 
> Then the constraint I mentioned at the start. That legacy stack had put a ceiling on us for years. Once the trust was real, I got the group GMs and our SVP of Engineering aligned that experience quality was a priority even where it meant building bespoke, and I got the HCM group GM and our CTO aligned on accelerating API development against the top user needs. That work is underway now.
> 
> Nobody had to be sold. It was a no-brainer at that point, because they knew I wasn't bullshitting them.

## 7 · What I got wrong

> One thing I'd do differently.
> 
> As part of the org change I elevated one of my senior directors of design to lead all of design. That was the wrong call.
> 
> He wasn't quite ready for that much scope. I also think the scope itself may be too much for most design leaders, which means the structure was part of my mistake, not just the person in it.
> 
> What it produced was uneven. Some design directors were underdeveloped and didn't get what they needed from him. Others were performing above their level and didn't get the chance to step up, because the layer above them was full.
> 
> I put a person in a role instead of designing the role and then finding the person.

# CLOSE · 2.5 minutes

> I'll leave you with why I do this.
> 
> Early in my career I was running a research session. The participant was impeccably dressed, beautifully styled, clearly someone who took care with how she showed up in the world. We gave her a task on a product I'd already told the engineer wasn't going to work. I sat behind the glass and watched.
> 
> She tried. She tried again. She got quieter.
> 
> Then she reached up, grabbed her hair, pulled it down in front of her face, and let go.
> 
> Made-up task. Fake room. Nothing at stake. And it made her want to hide.
>
