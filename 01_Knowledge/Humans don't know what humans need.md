There is a dimension to the AI challenge that aviation never had to contend with at this scale and it makes the AI design problem fundamentally harder.

We are bad at knowing ourselves.

Research consistently shows that humans have limited ability to accurately recall past behavior and almost no ability to predict their future preferences. We think we know what we want. We often do not. We think we will behave a certain way. We frequently do not. Our stated preferences diverge from our revealed ones. Our current priorities are poor predictors of our priorities six months from now.

Yet the most powerful AI agents will be built on models of us — learned from our behavior, our patterns, our signals over time. A health agent monitoring sleep and activity for months. An educational assistant that refines its approach based on what works and what doesn't. Systems that know our patterns better than we consciously do.

This is where the trust challenge gets genuinely new. In aviation, the autopilot is trying to follow a flight plan. In agentic AI, the system is trying to follow you — a moving, contradictory, context-dependent target that you yourself cannot fully articulate.

The appropriate response is not to slow down the capability development. It is to be rigorous about what these systems expose to human understanding, what authority they hold, and what recourse exists when their model of you diverges from who you actually are right now.
