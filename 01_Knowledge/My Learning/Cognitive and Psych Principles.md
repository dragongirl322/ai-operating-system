# Cognitive and Psychological Principles

A reference list of cognitive biases, heuristics, and psychological effects relevant to decision-making, UX, and behavioral design.

## Memory & Attention

- **Von Restorff Effect (Isolation Effect):** When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered.
- **Serial Position Effect:** People tend to remember the first and last items in a series.
- **Primacy Effect:** Items near the beginning of a sequence are easier to recall; combined with recency, items in the middle are the least likely to be remembered.
- **Context Effect:** Cognition and memory are dependent on context — out-of-context memories are harder to retrieve (e.g., recall accuracy for a work-related memory is lower at home, and vice versa).
- **Mere Exposure Effect:** The tendency to express undue liking for things merely because of familiarity with them.
- **Priming:** An implicit memory effect in which exposure to one stimulus influences the response to another. For example, *NURSE* is recognized more quickly following *DOCTOR* than following *BREAD*.
- **Attribute Priming:** Talking to customers about a certain attribute of a product can make them desire that attribute more.

## Decision-Making & Choice

- **Hick's Law:** The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.
- **Paradox of Choice:** Too much choice leaves people bewildered and unsatisfied after making a decision.
- **Compromise Effect:** Consumers are more likely to choose the middle option of a selection set rather than the extreme options (e.g., picking the mid-priced car between a basic and a fully-loaded model).
- **Decoy Effect (Attraction / Asymmetric Dominance Effect):** Consumers tend to shift preference between two options when a third, asymmetrically dominated option is introduced.
- **Distinction Bias:** The tendency to view two options as more dissimilar when evaluating them simultaneously than when evaluating them separately.
- **Satisficing:** Settling for something that may not be the best possible choice but meets essential needs (a portmanteau of *satisfy* and *suffice*).
- **Bounded Rationality:** Human decision-making is limited by available information, cognitive capacity, and time. Designs should simplify choices and provide sensible defaults to reduce cognitive load.
- **Approach-Avoidance Conflict:** A goal has both positive and negative characteristics simultaneously, creating internal conflict over whether to proceed.

## Heuristics & Mental Shortcuts

- **Representative Heuristic:** Classifying something as belonging to a category based on its similarity to a typical case from that category.
- **Anchoring:** Relying too heavily on the first piece of information offered (the "anchor") when making decisions.
- **Availability Heuristic:** Overestimating the likelihood of events with greater "availability" in memory — influenced by recency, unusualness, or emotional charge.
- **Focusing Effect:** Placing too much importance on one aspect of an event.

## Loss, Risk & Value

- **Loss Aversion:** People strongly prefer avoiding losses to acquiring equivalent gains.
- **Sunk Cost Fallacy:** Continuing an activity because of previously invested resources, without considering overall losses from further investment.
- **Endowment Effect:** People demand much more to give up an object than they would be willing to pay to acquire it.
- **Scarcity:** Things are more attractive when their availability is limited, or when we stand to lose the opportunity to acquire them on favorable terms.
- **Framing:** People react to a choice differently depending on how it is presented (as a loss or a gain). They tend to avoid risk under a positive frame and seek risk under a negative frame.
- **Temporal Discounting:** Humans respond far more to immediate rewards than to delayed ones. Offered $10 now or $15 next month, most choose the first option.

## Self-Perception & Social Influence

- **Overconfidence Effect:** Humans tend to overrate their own performance across many tasks — not as intentional bragging, but as a genuine ego-protective miscalibration.
- **Social Norms:** The urge to conform is more powerful than most realize.
  - **Injunctive norms:** what society hopes you'll do.
  - **Descriptive norms:** what society actually does.
- **Halo Effect:** A person's (or product's) positive or negative traits "spill over" into perceptions of unrelated traits.
- **Negativity Bias:** Humans recall unpleasant memories more readily than positive ones.

## Experience & Emotion

- **Peak-End Rule (Kahneman):** When forming impressions, humans unconsciously rely on the most emotionally intense moments and the end of an experience.
  - *Classic demonstration:* Participants who held their hand in 14° ice water for 60 seconds and then slightly warmer water for 30 seconds rated the experience as **less painful** than those exposed to 14° water for only 30 seconds.
- **Hedonic Adaptation:** Humans quickly return to a stable level of happiness despite major positive or negative life events. In UX, surprise and delight become expectation over time.
- **Cognitive Ease vs. Strain:** Text in a clear font, repeated, or primed is processed with ease. Poor fonts, faint colors, complex wording, or a bad mood induce strain. Even physical states (smiling vs. frowning) shift this.
- **Endowed Progress Effect:** Motivation increases as people believe they are nearing a goal.

## Research & Study Biases

- **Hawthorne Effect:** Participants change behavior because they know they are being observed.
- **Order Bias:** Earlier tasks or questions "teach" participants and influence later responses.
- **Consistency Bias:** Participants try to appear consistent across their responses.
- **Sponsor Bias:** Answers or behavior change based on who is sponsoring the study.
- **Experimenter Bias:** Experimenters tend to favor data that agree with their expectations and downgrade data that conflict with them.

## Interface & Interaction Design

Principles especially relevant to UX and AI system design.

- **Doherty Threshold:** Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace where neither has to wait on the other — generally **under 400 ms** response time.
- **Jakob's Law:** People scan screens based on past experience and expectations. Follow well-known layouts and patterns where possible so users can leverage existing mental models.
- **Progressive Disclosure:** Reduce cognitive load by surfacing only the information people need at the moment, allowing them to click or expand to learn more if they want.

## Learning & Conditioning

Principles for shaping behavior and supporting skill acquisition — useful when designing AI systems that teach, guide, or adapt to users.

- **Operant Conditioning:** Reinforce desired behaviors with positive feedback and rewards (and discourage undesired ones with negative consequences).
- **Classical Conditioning:** Pair AI interactions with positive stimuli so users come to associate the system itself with positive feelings.
- **Observational Learning:** People learn by watching others. Let users learn by observing AI demonstrations, examples, or how other users accomplish tasks.
- **Scaffolding:** Provide structured guidance and support — especially for new or complex tasks — and gradually remove it as the user gains competence.
