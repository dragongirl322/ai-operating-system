- Use the following mental model when you’re mapping influence across your organization: • Inner circle: Direct boss, executive sponsors, decision-makers. • Middle circle: Cross-functional peers, collaborators, secondary approvers. • Outer circle: Legal, finance, HR, tech, PR, and anyone else who can delay or derail your plan if they’re caught off guard. People in your middle and outer circles are often overlooked, and that’s exactly where influence starts to break down. Think of your map like a radar. If someone’s outside the view, they can still take you down, so pull them into the process early. ([Location 1589](https://readwise.io/to_kindle?action=open&asin=B0FZWX57W5&location=1589))

- If you want a fast-track way to figure out what matters to your stakeholders, use the SCARF model, a neuroscience-backed framework developed by David Rock. SCARF stands for status, certainty, autonomy, relatedness, and fairness, which are five things our brains crave and react to when threatened. Each stakeholder is wired a little differently. Some want recognition, others want control, and still others want to feel included. Figure out what drives and triggers them, then tailor your approach accordingly. Influence gets a whole lot easier when you understand the psychology behind it. ([Location 1596](https://readwise.io/to_kindle?action=open&asin=B0FZWX57W5&location=1596))

One study found a 13% improvement in accuracy and decision-making when under pressure (Journal of Applied Sport Psychology). • People who prepare for negotiations feel 50% more confident and are far more likely to reach win-win outcomes (Forbes / Wharton School of Business). • Executive leaders who report feeling confident in key meetings cite preparation as the #1 factor. Not charisma or tenure—prep (Center for Creative Leadership). Preparation is confidence in motion. - The following are the sneaky saboteurs that chip away at your self-belief: • Perfectionism: The lie that you must be flawless before you begin. It keeps you stuck in prep mode, waiting for the “perfect” time that never comes. o Fix it: Focus on progress over perfection, ship it messy, and learn as you go. Done is better than perfect because perfect doesn’t exist. Confidence comes from action, not polishing. • Comparison: Looking left and right instead of forward. Someone else’s highlight reel can make you question your path, especially when you forget that yours is still unfolding. o Fix it: Use comparison as inspiration, not indictment. Ask, “What can I learn?” not, “What am I lacking?” Then, zoom out. Everyone is on their own timeline, including you. • Imposter syndrome: The nagging belief that you’re one mistake away from being “found out.” Even high performers get it. Confidence means reminding yourself that you earned your seat. o Fix it: Keep a “wins” file with evidence you’ve earned your seat. Confidence isn’t pretending you know everything; it’s trusting you’ll figure it out. • Overthinking: The mental hamster wheel, spinning so fast on what-ifs that you forget what you actually know. Preparation helps, but so does pausing and trusting your instincts. o Fix it: Replace “What if I fail?” with “What if this works?” Get out of your head and into action. Clarity comes from movement, not mental spirals. • Negative self-talk: That inner critic who really needs to take several seats. Pay attention to your internal monologue because the words you say to yourself shape what you believe. o Fix it: Give your inner critic a name, and then ignore it. Talk to yourself as you would to a friend. Would you call her a fraud? Didn’t think so. ([Location 1907](https://readwise.io/to_kindle?action=open&asin=B0FZWX57W5&location=1907))
