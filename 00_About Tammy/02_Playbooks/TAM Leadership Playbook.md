# Craft: The Foundation Beneath the Lenses

Craft is what separates suitable work from excellent work. It is the commitment to mastery, the discipline of iteration, the balance of beauty and function, and the intention behind every design decision. Without craft, strategy produces nothing worth experiencing.

_But craft is an enabler, not the destination. The business value of design does not live in the quality of the artifact; it lives in what the artifact makes possible for the people who experience it and the organizations that deliver it._

Craft in service of the North Star. That is the only craft worth pursuing.

**Principles that govern craft:**

·       Honor people: At the heart of every design decision is people. To craft is to understand their goals, behaviors, and needs deeply. Great design begins and ends with people in mind.

·       Strive for Mastery: Mastery is a journey, not a destination. It is the constant pursuit of growth, pushing boundaries, refining skills, and valuing the “near-perfect” as a catalyst for deeper understanding and innovation.

·       Cherish Iteration: The journey to excellence is non-linear. Craft demands resilience, curiosity, and a willingness to refine until we achieve clarity, balance, and utility.

·       Balance Beauty and Functionality: Craft thrives where form meets function. True excellence lies in creating experiences that are as delightful as they are effective, where beauty enhances purpose.

·       Catalyze Collaboration: Our unique strength lies in fostering collaboration that revolves around the human experience. We act as catalysts, bringing together diverse perspectives and disciplines, united by a shared focus on understanding and serving people.

·       Create with Intent: Every choice—big or small—must align with purpose. Craft is intentional, ensuring each detail contributes meaningfully to the whole.

# The Operating Model

## TRENCHANT — Strategic Clarity

_Strategic clarity that cuts through complexity and defines direction._

### 1. Vision and Objective Setting

Define a compelling vision aligned to business goals. Translate vision into strategic objectives and measurable results. Support teams in determining how to achieve them; provide direction on the how when necessary — for both the UX org and partners in product and engineering.

### 2. Enterprise Alignment

Understand and align to the vision and objectives of the business. Map how each major business pillar connects to that vision. Ensure design strategy serves and strengthens every pillar, not just the product organization.

### 3. Experience as Business Strategy

Experience exists everywhere, not just in product. Customers experience the organization through advertising, sales practices, physical interactions, digital interactions, and everything in between. Whether those experiences are deliberate or accidental decides how much value customers receive.

### 4. The Full Experience Ecosystem

Account for what must be true across the entire customer journey:

•    Brand side — awareness, belief

•    Product side — functional quality, usability, appeal, value

•    Go-to-market side — how the brand shows up before and after the product interaction

### 5. Prioritization Frameworks

Help the business make better prioritization decisions through evidence and frameworks. Quantify and describe the outcomes customers value most. Assess organizational performance compared to competitors on those outcomes. Show where the organization has the highest likelihood of winning. Connect design investment directly to strategic opportunity.

### 6. Platform Thinking and Network Effects

Apply platform principles and network effects to experience strategy. Map every constituent in the ecosystem — internal teams, external customers, partners — and what each values. Design for how value compounds as the system grows, not just how individual interactions perform.

## ANCHORED — Inquiry and Evidence

_Inquiry and evidence that ground every decision in what is true._

### 1. Constituent Intelligence

Map and deeply understand every constituent in the ecosystem: customers, internal teams, partners. Define what each constituent needs, values, and experiences. This is not a one-time exercise; it is an evergreen system.

### 2. Outcome-Based Prioritization

Use evidence to identify the outcomes customers value most. Assess current performance against those outcomes relative to competitors. Name where the organization has the highest probability of winning. Connect those opportunities directly to business prioritization decisions.

### 3. Brand Effectiveness Framework

A strong brand is an attractor that drives growth. Measure and manage:

•    Known — is the brand recognized?

•    Distinct — does it stand apart from competitors?

•    Understood — is the messaging clear?

•    Believed — is the messaging credible?

•    Preferred — do people choose it over alternatives?

_These are not soft metrics. They are leading indicators of business growth._

### 4. Product Experience Hierarchy

Not all experience quality is equal. There is a clear hierarchy:

•    Functional, reliable, secure — table stakes; without these nothing else matters

•    Usable and accessible — necessary for any customer to derive value

•    Appealing — drives preference and positive emotion

•    Loved and preferred — the compounding result of brand and product excellence coming together

_Diagnostic decisions must respect this hierarchy. Fix lower levels before improving higher ones._

### 5. Intelligence Systems

Build and maintain systems that house what the organization knows about customers, partners, and the competitive landscape. In the age of AI, these systems must be queryable for immediate, actionable intelligence. Ground them in grounded theory method. Make them accessible across the enterprise, not siloed within the design organization.

### 6. Measurement and Effectiveness Tracking

Maintain an evergreen system for measuring overall brand and product quality. Track the effectiveness of ways of working — is the organization becoming more experience and design obsessed? Where are the gaps? How do they get closed? What is measured gets managed.

# The Accomplishment Framework

Every career story follows four moves in sequence:

•    DIAGNOSE — What was broken, missing, or underperforming? What did the evidence show?

•    ARCHITECT — What framework, system, or strategy did you define to address it?

•    ALIGN — Who did you need to bring with you, and how did you get them there?

•    DELIVER — What changed as a measurable result?

**Mapped to TAM:**

•    TRENCHANT stories — lead with the business problem, not the design solution. The design work is the method. The business outcome is the headline.

•    ANCHORED stories — lead with what you did not know, what you built to find out, and what became possible because you now knew it.

•    MEANINGFUL stories — lead with the human condition that was broken, what you designed to fix it, and how people responded.

•    STEEL THREAD stories — lead with the outcome that surprised people. The business result they did not expect a design leader to deliver.

•    COHERENT ARCHITECTURE stories — lead with what was not possible before you built it, and what became possible after.

# Career Stories

## Story 1: The Expedia Flywheel

_Steel Thread / Trenchant_

Expedia was shifting to a value-based strategy, and I got ahead of it. I led research to identify the top value drivers for travelers and partners, then went to the SVP of Travel Partner product with an uncomfortable truth: partner satisfaction was declining because we were spreading too thin. I recommended we pick two or three value drivers and go deep exclusively. He agreed. We executed and partner satisfaction increased after three consecutive years of decline.

But there was a second part to my role that I found equally compelling. Our comms team and product leadership repeatedly asked me to present to travel and advertising partners: Emirates, Delta, Marriott, Hilton, major advertisers, not to talk about the partner experience, but to show them what we were doing on the traveler side. The argument was simple: here is the evidence-based approach we take to designing high-value, appealing experiences for travelers. Here is why travelers choose our platform and keep coming back. That is why being on Expedia means more sales for you.

I was feeding the flywheel from both ends — fixing the partner experience on one side and converting our traveler experience investment into partner commercial confidence on the other.

## Story 2: The Workday Admin Vision

_Trenchant / Steel Thread_

I noticed something that nobody had formally named: customers were not adopting functionality we had improved, which meant we were not getting credit for the investments we were making. And underneath that was a more complex problem. Every Workday customer has a unique permutation of products deployed and configured, which means their experience of our product is fundamentally different from what we designed. The admin layer — the people responsible for deployment, configuration, and maintenance — was the leverage point nobody was focused on.

I made a decision to do something about it before anyone asked me to. I aligned with key leaders across product, CX, and sales around the value of a unified vision, which did not exist, and recommended we start with research. We went deep on how admins work and found two critical outcomes they need to achieve and the specific points in their journey where things break down.

What happened next is what I am most proud of. I did not just deliver a design vision. I changed how the sales team talks to customer admins about experience improvements, which has driven adoption of key features over the past six months. I influenced CX to be more tolerant of short-term friction from on-by-default feature releases in service of long-term adoption. And we delivered our first agentic experience addressing one of those critical admin journeys.

At a recent CIO advisory council and analyst event, that release was described as one of the most impactful things Workday has done in years. That is not a design compliment. That is a business compliment, which is exactly what design should be earning.

## Story 3: The Expedia Cohesion Strategy

_Coherent Architecture / Anchored_

After an expensive advertising campaign underperformed, our Chief Product Officer came to me to figure out why. That question alone told me something — the answer was not going to be found in the media buy or the creative brief.

Our research revealed something more fundamental. Travelers could not recognize Expedia as the advertiser. Not just that the ads were not resonating, they literally could not connect the advertising to the brand. And when they could not recognize who was talking to them, nothing else mattered: the emotional appeal, the message, the offer. The brand and the product were telling two completely different stories and travelers could feel the disconnect even if they could not name it.

Around the same time, the President of Brand Expedia had discovered Byron Sharp's How Brands Grow and was intrigued by the idea of growth through increased brand awareness. I read the book and saw a more specific opportunity, brand distinction. Not just being known but being recognizable and sticky in ways competitors could not easily replicate. I led efforts to measure and increase the distinctiveness of key brand elements across Brand Expedia and our subsidiary brands (Travelocity, Orbitz, etc.) which required influence across both marketing and product simultaneously.

This became the foundation of our cohesion strategy. I was asked to define what cohesion actually means as a measurable business quality — not just a design principle — and to lead efforts to build for it across product, advertising, and support. We built the measurement framework from scratch because cohesion had never been formally tracked before.

The results were significant at scale. Brand awareness increased over 5% in a single quarter. Brand perceptions improved by 8%. The equity of flagship logos and taglines for both Expedia and Travelocity moved further into the high value quadrant. Package bookings increased 3% year-over-year. A new CEO and business restructuring eventually revamped priorities — but what that tells you is that this kind of work requires both the right architecture and the organizational conditions to sustain it. Building both is part of how I think about the role.

## Story 4: The Workday AI Systems

_Ready When the Moment Arrives / Anchored_

When ChatGPT arrived in late 2022 I decided we were not going to wait for the organization to tell us to get ready. We started at once by defining human-centered AI guidelines, establishing UX paradigms for distinct AI modalities, building interaction patterns into our design system, and fostering a culture of learning, including holding our own AI summit, before the organization had formally started building agents.

The reason that mattered is what happened next. When Workday started building agents, we were not starting from zero. We had frameworks for showing which agent opportunities were worth pursuing and which were not. We had patterns. We had measurement infrastructure already in place. We had a team that knew how to work in this medium.

Our first organic Self Service agent was delivered in three months because of fantastic cross-functional efforts. Historically that would have taken well over a year. When evidence from our experience rubric showed the agent was not ready to ship, I influenced the President of Product and Technology to delay the release, we addressed the quality issues and shipped something the market could trust. Usage increased 73% in three weeks as a direct result of improvements informed by real-time data our team had already built the capability to capture.

The measurement foundation went further than agent improvement. It became the data infrastructure needed to support Workday's shift to consumption-based pricing, a fundamental change in how the business goes to market. Without it, the company would have had to pause and build that capability from scratch, at significant cost in time and resources. We were ready because we had seen it coming.

At a recent analyst event, Workday showcased agents across product areas, user types, and acquired products. Analysts noted the solutions and the narrative were cohesive and compelling. My team played a primary role in making that true, including a UX Studio that leads narrative definition and demo refinement. That is not a coincidence. That is what systems of scale built deliberately, in advance, make possible.

# The Hard Lesson

Early in Workday's AI strategy, we had evidence that the quantity over quality approach was not going to work. Our research showed most customers were not ready to adopt AI at the pace we were building it, that the agents being prioritized were not likely to deliver meaningful value, and that moving too fast would result in poor experiences that would destroy user trust before it was ever established. I was also hearing privately from product leaders that their teams simply did not have the skills to build what was being asked of them.

I tried to make the case to our CPO. But I made a mistake in how I approached it. I was fragmented — raising concerns one at a time through written communication instead of pulling together a clear, systematic narrative of the problem, the risks, and a proposed solution. I should have asked for in-person time, traveled to make the meeting happen, and walked in with a complete picture that had input from product leaders who trusted me but were not comfortable delivering the message themselves.

The CPO could not convince the CEO and chairman that the approach was sound. He was eventually let go. The teams tried and failed to build agents they were not ready to build.

What I learned — or more accurately, what I am still learning — is that having the right evidence and the right read is not enough at the highest levels. The narrative architecture of how you deliver a difficult truth matters as much as the truth itself. I was in a position to give that CPO information his own leaders were not giving him. I did not deliver it in a way that gave it the weight it deserved.

Thank you. We are going to be fine.

I am Tam. I have spent my career leading design and experience at some of the world's most complex organizations — Expedia Group, where I helped reposition a global travel brand through emotion science, and Workday, where I am building the infrastructure that makes enterprise AI trustworthy at scale. My work has been featured in the New York Times and Bloomberg. I have presented to CIOs, oCHROs, and some of the world's largest travel and technology brands. I am here today because I have something to say that I think most people in rooms like this need to hear.

**PATHOS — Move Them**

Early in my career I was running a research session with a female participant. She was impeccably dressed — beautifully styled, clearly someone who took care in how she presented herself to the world. We gave her a task to complete on a product I had already told the engineer was not going to work. I sat behind the glass and watched.

She tried. She tried again. She got quieter. And then — at the moment she hit the wall — she reached up, grabbed her beautifully done hair, pulled it down in front of her face, and let it go.

I knew. The evidence was clear. But more than that — something shifted in me permanently.

If something this simple, this mundane, in a contrived research setting could produce that level of frustration in a person — what must it be like for people in situations that actually matter? People trying to access their healthcare. Manage their finances. Do their jobs. Take care of their families. What are we doing to them every single day with the products and systems we build?

From that moment forward I knew exactly why I was in this field.

**LOGOS — Give Them the Logic**

Here is what I need you to reconsider before you leave today.

Saying that UX and design are only about the way a product looks and feels is like saying a woman's place is in the kitchen. It is wrong. It is limiting. And it is costing you.

Your customers are interacting with your brand and your products right now — whether you are being intentional about it or not. Every interaction is either designed or accidental. Every touchpoint either tells a coherent story or a fragmented one. Every experience either moves someone closer to what they need or further away from it.

The organizations that understand this — that treat experience as a strategic business capability rather than a finishing coat of paint — are the ones that earn repeat customers, command premium pricing, and build brands people actually love.

The measure of great design is not the artifact. It is what the artifact makes possible.

**_Every person who experiences what we build should find themselves more capable, more confident, and more free._**

That is not an aspiration. That is a standard. And it is the only standard worth building to."
